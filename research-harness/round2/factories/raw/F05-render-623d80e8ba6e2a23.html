Building LLM Judges to Judge LLMs

Sachet BangiaShaiyon Hariri

We recently launched Ramp SWE Bench V2! At Ramp, we spend millions of dollars on tokens to produce and review code internally, and now for our customers through Router. We’re in the business of saving time and money, which means we care deeply about spending those dollars well. So we built our own benchmark to understand how different models perform on the specific coding tasks our engineers delegate to AI, and use that evidence to guide our spending.

A reliable coding benchmark needs a reliable way to distinguish correct solutions from plausible ones. Figuring out whether code satisfies the user’s request is harder than it might seem — this was the central challenge we tackled going from V1 to V2. The new version has six times as many benchmark tasks (!) and evaluates models on their native harnesses as well as a common harness, but the biggest change was how we grade solutions. Given the scale of our AI spend, we gave this decision a lot of thought.

Like earlier iterations of many coding benchmarks, we previously graded solutions by running them against a predefined suite of tests. We have since replaced those tests with LLM judges. That might seem like a strange trade: tests give us explicit, repeatable checks, while open-ended LLM judgments are notoriously flaky and sensitive to how a question is framed. This post explains why we made the switch and how we wrangled these non-deterministic beasts into giving us consistent judgments that we trust.

Where Tests Fall Short, and Why LLM Judges Aren’t a Simple Fix

Ramp SWE-Bench is built from real engineering work at Ramp. We turn a subset of Inspect sessions that ended in merged pull requests into benchmark tasks, asking new models to solve the same requests (Inspect is Ramp’s collaborative, cloud-based coding agent; you can sign up for early access here!). The code that passed engineer review and went live in production gives us a reference solution, or gold patch. Previously, we used the accompanying tests in the gold patch to grade new solutions.

The tests merged with those patches often rejected valid solutions because they expected a particular implementation. These overspecified tests became a bottleneck: for V1, we painstakingly audited and repaired test suites, and struggled to keep up. Similar test failures are documented in OpenAI’s audit of SWE-Bench Pro. Aggressively filtering out tasks with overly restrictive tests left us with a set more vulnerable to the opposite problem: underspecified tests that checked some requirements but missed others, letting incomplete or incorrect solutions slip through the cracks.

LLM judges, increasingly common across benchmarks, offer a more flexible and scalable way to grade solutions. But we cannot simply hand an LLM the task and a candidate solution and ask: “Is this correct?” There is little reason to take those answers at face value, particularly if we’re asking less capable models to grade the work of more capable ones.

We have something more concrete to work with: the engineer-reviewed gold patch. We can take a more directed approach: give an LLM judge the task prompt, relevant repository context, and the gold and candidate patches, then ask whether the patches implement the same requested behavior. This seemingly straightforward question — whether two code patches do the same thing — turns out to have surprisingly model-dependent answers. As a test, we had seven judge models evaluate solutions from twenty solver models across 72 tasks from Ramp SWE-Bench V1. Below, each point represents one solver assessed by one judge, comparing its test-based pass rate with its judge-based pass rate.

Reassuringly, the judges generally agreed with the tests about which models were stronger: using a majority vote across the seven judges, the correlation between solver pass rates under the two approaches was 0.85. But individual judges gave enormously different scores: Opus passed nearly 60% of comparisons, while Sol and Luna passed only about 25%. Nearly half of comparisons had at least one dissenting judge.

All seven judges were also substantially more strict than the tests. In the task-level plot below, the bottom-right points are tasks where solutions often passed the tests but rarely satisfied the judges. When we investigated those cases, we found tests passing solutions that missed task requirements. This leniency made Ramp SWE-Bench V1 appear more saturated than it actually was.

Getting the LLM Judges to Agree

Our goal was to build a verification process where, to the extent possible, a solution’s grade would not hinge on which model judged it. To get there, we needed to understand why the judges disagreed. Sometimes one spotted a difference that another missed. More often, both noticed the same differences but disagreed about their relevance to the task. We tried clarifying the instructions (aka prompt tuning), but that barely moved the needle. We also experimented with task-specific guidance, but tailoring prompts to individual examples risked overfitting and wouldn’t scale.

We started making progress when we separated identifying differences between the two patches from judging whether those differences mattered to the task. The original approach bundled both into a single judgment, making it harder to distinguish something a judge missed from something it considered unimportant. Separating those steps lets the judges assess the same evidence and work through their reasons for disagreeing, rather than simply cast conflicting votes. Putting the Pipeline to the Test

Here are results from 360 comparisons (spanning 72 tasks and 20 solver models, with five models evaluated per task and 18 tasks per model). We show Sol and Opus’ judgments because the two displayed markedly different grading tendencies in the original one-shot experiment, comparing those judgments with their independent first votes in the new pipeline and their decisions after deliberation.

MetricOriginal panelNew pipeline: first voteAfter deliberation
Sol passes89/360 (24.7%)100/360 (27.8%)116/360 (32.2%)
Opus passes240/360 (66.7%)174/360 (48.3%)135/360 (37.5%)
Sol/Opus agreement195/360 (54.2%)275/360 (76.4%)337/360 (93.6%)

Agreement rose from 54% to 76% before any discussion, and to 94% after deliberation. Having both judges work off the same list and assess each difference individually closed much of the gap, and the deliberation narrowed it further.

Of course, while greater judge agreement is encouraging, it does not by itself prove that the LLMs are converging to the truth. As a sanity check, we did some small-scale human labelling to validate the accuracy of the pipeline’s judgments. We asked engineers behind the original PRs for each task to evaluate whether the identified differences were material enough that they would not merge the candidate patch without changes: the engineers agreed with nearly 90% of the task-level verdicts.

Given that our grading approach rests on direct comparison with the gold patch, we were naturally concerned that a candidate would score better if it came from the same model provider as the gold patch. We found no evidence of such an advantage: models were neither rewarded nor penalized for sharing a provider with the model that produced the gold patch.
