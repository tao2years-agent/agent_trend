Building LLM Judges to Judge LLMs
Sachet Bangia
Shaiyon Hariri
We recently launched
Ramp SWE Bench V2
! At Ramp, we spend millions of dollars on tokens to produce and review code internally, and now for our customers through
Router
. We’re in the business of saving time and money, which means we care deeply about spending those dollars well. So we built our own benchmark to understand how different models perform on the specific coding tasks our engineers delegate to AI, and use that evidence to guide our spending.
A reliable coding benchmark needs a reliable way to distinguish correct solutions from plausible ones. Figuring out whether code satisfies the user’s request is harder than it might seem — this was the central challenge we tackled going from V1 to V2. The new version has six times as many benchmark tasks (!) and evaluates models on their native harnesses as well as a common harness, but the biggest change was how we grade solutions. Given the scale of our AI spend, we gave this decision a lot of thought.
Like earlier iterations of many coding benchmarks, we previously graded solutions by running them against a predefined suite of tests. We have since replaced those tests with LLM judges. That might seem like a strange trade: tests give us explicit, repeatable checks, while open-ended LLM judgments are notoriously flaky and sensitive to how a question is framed. This post explains why we made the switch and how we wrangled these non-deterministic beasts into giving us consistent judgments that we trust.
Where Tests Fall Short, and Why LLM Judges Aren’t a Simple Fix
Ramp SWE-Bench is built from real engineering work at Ramp. We turn a subset of Inspect sessions that ended in merged pull requests into benchmark tasks, asking new models to solve the same requests (Inspect is Ramp’s collaborative, cloud-based coding agent; you can
sign up for early access here
!). The code that passed engineer review and went live in production gives us a reference solution, or
gold patch
. Previously, we used the accompanying tests in the gold patch to grade new solutions.
The tests merged with those patches often rejected valid solutions because they expected a particular implementation. These
overspecified
tests became a bottleneck: for
V1
, we painstakingly audited and repaired test suites, and struggled to keep up. Similar test failures are documented in
OpenAI’s audit of SWE-Bench Pro
. Aggressively filtering out tasks with overly restrictive tests left us with a set more vulnerable to the opposite problem:
underspecified
tests that checked some requirements but missed others, letting incomplete or incorrect solutions slip through the cracks.
LLM judges, increasingly common across benchmarks, offer a more flexible and scalable way to grade solutions. But we cannot simply hand an LLM the task and a candidate solution and ask: “Is this correct?” There is little reason to take those answers at face value, particularly if we’re asking less capable models to grade the work of more capable ones.
We have something more concrete to work with: the engineer-reviewed gold patch. We can take a more directed approach: give an LLM judge the task prompt, relevant repository context, and the gold and candidate patches, then ask whether the patches implement the same requested behavior. This seemingly straightforward question — whether two code patches do the same thing — turns out to have surprisingly model-dependent answers. As a test, we had seven judge models evaluate solutions from twenty solver models across 72 tasks from Ramp SWE-Bench V1. Getting the LLM Judges to Agree
Our goal was to build a verification process where, to the extent possible, a solution’s grade would not hinge on which model judged it. To get there, we needed to understand why the judges disagreed. Sometimes one spotted a difference that another missed. More often, both noticed the same differences but disagreed about their relevance to the task. We tried clarifying the instructions (aka prompt tuning), but that barely moved the needle. We also experimented with task-specific guidance, but tailoring prompts to individual examples risked overfitting and wouldn’t scale.
We started making progress when we separated
identifying differences between the two patches
from
judging whether those differences mattered to the task
. The original approach bundled both into a single judgment, making it harder to distinguish something a judge missed from something it considered unimportant. Separating those steps lets the judges assess the same evidence and work through their reasons for disagreeing, rather than simply cast conflicting votes. Through successive experimentation, we arrived at the following pipeline:
LLM Judge Pipeline
(Hover for detail)
1. Extract differences
2. Deduplicate and consolidate
3. Judge each difference
4. Resolve disagreements
5. Verdict

. Having both judges work off the same list and assess each difference individually closed much of the gap, and the deliberation narrowed it further.
Of course, while greater judge agreement is encouraging, it does not by itself prove that the LLMs are converging to the truth. As a sanity check, we did some small-scale human labelling to validate the accuracy of the pipeline’s judgments. We asked engineers behind the original PRs for each task to evaluate whether the identified differences were material enough that they would not merge the candidate patch without changes: the engineers agreed with nearly 90% of the task-level verdicts.
Given that our grading approach rests on direct comparison with the gold patch, we were naturally concerned that a candidate would score better if it came from the same model provider as the gold patch. We found no evidence of such an advantage: models were neither rewarded nor penalized for sharing a provider with the model that produced the gold patch.
Example Tasks
Here are two tasks from the benchmark, each graded for a Claude Opus 5.5 solution. In the first, deliberation confirms a real gap. In the second, it resolves a disagreement in the candidate’s favor.
Card activation over SMS
Bill policy at the line level
Task prompt
Cardholders can activate a card by texting its last four digits, but a partially activated card only finishes activation through a separate flow that follows a declined transaction. Make the ordinary last-four SMS flow finish activating those cards too, without requiring a decline first.
How Opus’ patch differs from the original
Say a cardholder has two cards ending in the same four digits: one partially activated, one already active. The original change finishes activating the partial card. Opus replies “card already active,” so the partial card stays stuck.
Does that difference matter for the task?
Round 1 · Independent votes
Judge 1
Not material
No. The task doesn’t say what to do when two cards share the last four.
Judge 2
Material
Yes. The whole point is that texting the last four finishes activation, and here it doesn’t.
Round 2 · After deliberation
Judge 1
Material
Yes. The existing code already ignores active cards in this situation, so Opus shouldn’t stop there.
Judge 2
Material
Yes. Another active card doesn’t mean this one is done, so the cardholder stays stuck.
Fail.
Both judges agree this difference matters (along with three similar edge cases), so Opus fails.
