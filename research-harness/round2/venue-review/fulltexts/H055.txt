Mitigating the Risk of Defects and Improving Knowledge
Distribution with Code Reviewer Recommenders
MOHAMMADALI SEFIDI ESFAHANI, Concordia University, Canada
PETER C. RIGBY, Concordia University, Canada
Defects are inevitable in software projects, leading to increased maintenance costs, user dissatisfaction, and a
diminished software reputation. Code review is one of the most critical software quality assurance activities
that reduces software defects and improves software quality. Prior works have quantified the impact of
reviewer recommenders on the risk of inducing new defects based on the highest level of expertise among the
developers in the reviewer team. However, our analysis shows that prior work overestimates the safety of
a change and ignores the defect-finding effectiveness of the diverse knowledge of reviewers. In this study,
we incorporate the knowledge of the entire reviewer team into the author’s level of expertise and introduce
the novel Contribution-aware Changeset Safety Ratio (CCSR) outcome to assess the impact of code reviewer
recommenders on the risk of inducing defects more accurately.
   When a pull request is risky, a natural mitigation is to add an expert reviewer. We are unaware of any works
that have quantified the impact of adding a reviewer to risky PRs. We propose the novel AddExpertRec(𝐷𝑡 )
strategy that recommends an additional expert reviewer for defect-prone pull requests to reduce the likelihood
of introducing new defects when the risk is above the threshold 𝐷𝑡 . The simulation results show that
AddExpertRec(𝐷𝑡 ) can enhance the defect finding effectiveness of existing recommenders while still balancing
reviewer workload and spreading knowledge to reduce the impact of turnover. Ultimately, our results give
managers the ability to select a recommender strategy that best suits their project needs based on their
resource constraints. The scripts and data are available in our replication package.

1   Introduction
During software maintenance and evolution, developers constantly modify the source code to
introduce new features or fix reported defects. However, these modifications may cause new defects
in the software [30]. Software defects can slow down the development process [48], delay the
release of projects [24], increase the maintenance cost [7], reduce the product quality [16, 42],
diminish customer satisfaction [59], and undermine the software reputation [44].
   Code review is one of the most critical software activities that reduces software defects and
improves software quality [5, 34, 40, 65]. Despite the importance of Software Quality Assurance
activities for software quality [25, 70], development teams have limited resources and must wisely
allocate them to minimize the risk of incurring defects [27]. Finding reviewers with the time to

review [58, 63], especially in large projects with hundreds of developers and at large companies. In
a large project, the author of a PR may not know which reviewer or team to select, and this can
become an error-prone and time-consuming task [31]. Therefore, code reviewer recommendation
systems accommodate a wide range of knowledge among developers in large-scale projects and
help engineers find suitable reviewers for code changes [71].
   Prior works [41, 55] demonstrate the inherent potential of reviewer recommenders in knowledge
distribution and workload balancing in the development team. Moreover, Kononenko et al. [33]
highlight that expert developers are more proficient and effective in identifying defects during
the code review process. Upon this idea, Kazemi et al. [28] define the Changeset Safety Ratio (CSR)
outcome to quantify the impact of code reviewer recommenders on the risk of inducing defects
after integrating a patch to the codebase. However, their proposed CSR outcome only considers the
top reviewer’s knowledge and fails to account for the author’s expertise and the knowledge of the
entire reviewer team when measuring the risk reduction of inducing defects.
   In this study, we introduce the Contribution-aware Changeset Safety Ratio (CCSR) outcome to
assess the impact of code reviewer recommenders on the risk of inducing defects more accurately.
While measuring the risk of inducing defects, the CCSR outcome considers the combined knowledge
of the entire reviewer team and the author’s knowledge, rather than relying solely on the knowledge
of the top expert reviewer. We also propose a novel recommendation strategy that suggests an
additional expert reviewer for defect-prone pull requests to further enhance the defect-finding
effectiveness of the reviewer team during the code review process.
   Following prior works [20, 28, 41], we evaluate the efficacy of code reviewer recommenders with
the Expertise, GiniWorkload, KRT , and Rev++ outcomes. The Expertise outcome ensures that there
are still knowledgeable developers to review the code changes. The GiniWorkload outcome ensures
that the top reviewers are not unreasonably overworked because they are highly knowledgeable.
The Knowledge at Risk to Turnover (KRT ) outcome quantifies the knowledge distribution to ensure
that knowledge is adequately distributed during review to mitigate the risk of developer turnover.
The Rev++ outcome calculates the proportion of pull requests to which the recommenders add an
extra reviewer. We also evaluate our novel CCSR outcome to ensure that the assigned reviewers
are sufficiently expert to identify existing defects in a code change. Since each additional reviewer
represents work for the development team, the ideal recommender mitigates the risk of inducing
defects while minimizing knowledge loss and the amount of extra work for the team. To evaluate the
efficacy of the recommenders, we run simulations on historical reviews of three large open-source
projects.

1.1    Research Questions
We present our research questions and briefly summarize our findings below.
RQ1. CSR vs. CCSR: Which developers should be included in the Changeset Safety Ratio?
   Prior work [28] quantifies the impact of code reviewer recommenders on the risk of new defects
based on the knowledge of the reviewer with the most expertise for a PR. However, reviewers of
pull requests often have diverse types of expertise. For instance, one reviewer might be an expert
in networking, while another might be an expert in the file system. In addition to reviewers, the
authors’ knowledge is effective in reducing the risk of inducing future defects after integrating pull
requests. In this research question, we investigate the impact of reviewer recommenders on the
risk of inducing bugs by considering each developer’s expertise.
   Result summary: The top expert reviewer poorly represents the aggregated knowledge needed
for review, thereby overestimating the safety of the change. We introduce CCSR to factor in the
diverse expertise of all the review participants. When we look at each recommender based on

RQ2. Multiple Outcomes: In addition to the CCSR outcome, how well do the existing

   In the first RQ, we developed a more representative measure of change safety, but we did not

RQ3. AddExpertRec(𝐷𝑡 ): What is the impact of adding an extra reviewer to defect-prone

   In the defect prediction literature, researchers often suggest adding an extra reviewer to PRs that
are high risk, e.g., [27, 67, 69]. We are unaware of a study that has quantified the impact of adding
a reviewer. We present the novel AddExpertRec(𝐷𝑡 ) approach to mitigate the associated risk with
developer turnover, while maintaining the defect finding effectiveness by adding a reviewer. In this
approach, we leverage the RAR recommender [28] to suggest an expert reviewer for pull requests
when the probability of the change introducing a defect is at or above the threshold 𝐷𝑡 . We also
recommend replacing a reviewer with the top candidate for the other pull requests and show the
trade-off between ΔCCSR, ΔKRT, and Rev++ outcomes by varying 𝐷𝑡 .
   Results summary: The AddExpertRec(𝐷𝑡 ) approach can successfully balance the trade-off
between change safety and knowledge distribution by assigning an extra expert reviewer to defect-
prone PRs based on the likelihood of inducing new defects. By varying 𝐷𝑡 , we show how engineering
managers can trade off the assignment of extra experts based on their available resources and the
need to control the risk of field defects.

defects and reviewers’ extra work. In Section 6, we describe the threats to validity. In Section 7, we
position our findings in the research literature. In Section 8, we conclude our study.

3.4   Evaluation Outcomes
To evaluate the performance of reviewer recommenders, prior works [6, 22, 51, 66, 72, 77, 79]
make recommendations for each code change and compare their results with the reviewers who
actually performed the code review based on the top-k accuracy and Mean Reciprocal Rank (MRR)
metrics. However, Kovalenko et al. [35] highlight that reviewer recommenders rarely provide
additional value because they often suggest an obvious reviewer. This issue arises from outcome
measures that assume the actual reviewers are the best or ideal reviewers for the pull requests.
Dogan et al. [12] further argue that team leaders may fail to assign the ideal reviewers due to
unavailability or cognitive biases in decision-making. Kovalenko et al. [35] suggest considering
alternative perspectives and evaluating outcomes beyond merely predicting the actual reviewers.
Following this direction, researchers have proposed novel outcome measures, such as Expertise [41,
43], GiniWorkload [20], CSR [28], and KRT [55] to assess reviewer recommenders from diverse
perspectives. In this study, we measure five outcomes for each recommender to evaluate the impact
of code reviewer recommenders: the degree of reviewers’ Expertise, the Knowledge at Risk to
Turnover (KRT ), the GiniWorkload of reviewers, Contribution-aware Changeset Safety Ratio (CCSR),
and the proportion of pull requests to which an extra reviewer added (Rev++ ).

3.5    Simulation Setup
To investigate the impact of our proposed strategies on the outcome measures, we perform simula-
tions. The simulation involves sequentially making reviewer recommendations for each code review
in a project. For each code change, reviewer recommenders examine the files under review and
their full review history to rank the candidates based on their scoring functions. Furthermore, we
either add the top-recommended reviewer to the review process or suggest a replacement with one
of the actual reviewers. In this study, we replace only one developer to avoid drastically disrupting
the peer review process, as PR authors usually already know at least one expert candidate [35] to
review a code change. Some of the recommenders add an additional reviewer rather than replacing
a reviewer.
   Since alternative or additional reviewers do the code review, this change in the review process is
reflected in our simulation and outcome measures. For example, when a recommender replaces
DevA with DevB, we must transfer the workload and expertise from DevA to DevB, which will

affect the project’s distributions for future recommendations. These changes in turn impact the
outcome measures, e.g., by spreading knowledge. As a result, when DevA leaves the project, there
may now actually be someone who has expertise about the files, i.e. DevB.
   To run simulations, we extend SofiaWL’s replication package [21] and incorporate our strategies
into it. While working on this replication package, we observe that it excludes inactive developers
from the pool of potential developers and prevents recommenders from making stale recommen-
dations [29]. However, how long the developer will stay with the project is unknown and is
an inappropriate use of future information. After fixing this methodological issue, we run the
simulations and release the scripts and data in our replication package [14].
   The goal of this study is to compare the performance of reviewer recommenders and evaluate
their impact on outcome measures. Following Hajari et al. [20], in our simulations, we use seeded
random replacement of reviewers, which enables a fair comparison of reviewer recommenders
by ensuring that the same randomly selected reviewer is replaced by all recommenders. We have
over 58k reviews across three projects, making it unlikely that this random selection will lead to
systematic bias.
   In contrast to prior works [20, 28, 41] that maintain the same number of reviewers but randomly
replace one with the top recommendation, our novel strategies in this study assign an additional
expert reviewer for defect-prone pull requests. The simulation results for an ideal reviewer rec-
ommender in mitigating the risk of inducing future defects substantially increase the PRs’ safety
ratio (CCSR) and spread knowledge among the team by adding an extra reviewer to a minimal
percentage of pull requests (Rev++ ) and with the lowest impact on the reviewers’ GiniWorkload.

5    Discussion
We discuss the trade-offs an engineering manager could consider between improving the Safety Ratio
and knowledge distribution vs. the cost of adding extra reviewers. In Figure 2, we plot the tradeoff
between safety ratio, ΔCCSR, and knowledge distribution, ΔKRT, relative to the proportion of extra
reviews varying from 0% to 59.6% on average across projects. AcHRev and cHRev recommenders
suggest expert reviewers for code changes and are effective in reducing the risk of new defects
during the code review. However, the number of developers with high expertise is limited in
software projects [81], and these recommenders can not effectively distribute the knowledge at risk
among the development team. When we compare the simulation results for AcHRev with RAR in
Table 3, we observe that the RAR recommender spreads the knowledge of risky files more broadly,
ΔKRT of -5% vs. 49.9%, and achieves an acceptable increase in the safety ratio, ΔCCSR of 17.7% vs.
19.6%. At the replacement level, the results show that hybrid recommenders, such as RAR and Sofia,
which balance knowledge distribution and expert suggestion, are more effective in reducing the
risk of new defects and knowledge loss simultaneously.
   In this study, we leverage the RAR recommender in our novel AddExpertRec(𝐷𝑡 ) strategy to
increase the safety ratio in defect-prone pull requests by suggesting an extra expert reviewer for
them, while spreading the knowledge of risky files among the development team by replacing one of
the reviewers with the committed learner. Among our proposed AddExpertRec(𝐷𝑡 ) recommenders,
AddExpertRec(25) is the most aggressive recommender that achieves the ΔCCSR of 30.8% and
ΔKRT of -63.1%. These results are the highest enhancement in the CCSR and KRT outcomes among
all the studied recommenders. However, this enhancement comes with the cost of extra reviews
for the average 59.6% of pull requests, making this recommender infeasible for projects with
limited reviewers and budget constraints. Compared to the AcHRev recommender, AddExpertRec(50)
achieves a higher value for the ΔCCSR outcome, 22.1% vs. 19.6%, and substantially reduces the
KRT outcome with an average of -42.1% vs. 49.9% with the cost of adding an extra reviewer to the
average 23.9% of pull requests. The reason for the effective performance of the AddExpertRec(𝐷𝑡 )
strategy in knowledge distribution and mitigating the risk of new defects is that it balances between
suggesting experts and committed reviewers who are less likely to leave the projects.
   Our comparative analysis of the performance of the AddExpertRec(𝐷𝑡 ) strategy across projects
in Table 4 indicates that the nature of the projects significantly influences the outcome measures.
For instance, AddExpertRec(50) achieves the ΔCCSR of 27.1% and ΔKRT of -40% in the Kubernetes
project by adding an extra reviewer to 28.1% of pull requests. In contrast, this recommender achieves
the ΔCCSR of 25.2%, ΔKRT of -28.4%, and Rev++ of 31.6% in the Rust project. This comparison
highlights that adding extra reviewers to more pull requests does not always lead to a higher safety
ratio and more extensive knowledge distribution. Other aspects of projects, such as reviewers’
level of expertise, dependencies on key developers, code complexity, and knowledge distribution
cultures, are interesting avenues for future work.
   Ultimately, it will be up to managers to determine which approach best suits their specific
context, considering factors such as project importance, available budget, and resource constraints.

hybrid recommenders like RAR to maintain the safety ratio, while distributing knowledge among
the development team and assigning fewer extra reviews.

6 Threats to Validity
Construct Validity. Following prior works on code reviewer recommendation [17, 19, 45, 53, 55,
66, 78], we just cover the most basic source of knowledge, i.e. source code files, and consider all files
to be equally important in this study. However, knowledge is contained in other documents and at
various unit levels, like commit messages and project documentation. Moreover, the knowledge
acquired by a reviewer will be different from that of the author. The author will typically have
more detailed knowledge, while an expert reviewer may have more expertise in other modules
and dependencies. In this work, we follow prior studies [20, 28, 41] and assume that both authors
and reviewers are knowledgeable about the files they have previously edited or reviewed. While
measuring the CxFactor metric as a degree of developers’ expertise, we assign the same weights to

Proc. ACM Softw. Eng., Vol. 3, No. FSE, Article FSE008. Publication date: July 2026.
Mitigating the Risk of Defects and Improving Knowledge Distribution with Code Reviewer Recommenders         FSE008:15


their review comments, commits, and recency of their contributions. Understanding the importance
of various types and degrees of knowledge that authors and reviewers gain from their contributions
is outside the scope of this work and is an interesting future work.
   Our novel CCSR outcome measures the Changeset Safety Ratio based on the reviewers knowledge,
author level of expertise, and PRs defect proneness. In addition to the linear summation of reviewer
and author knowledge, we also ran simulations using alternative aggregation functions, including
max, mean, and median. We do not report these results in the main paper due to space limitations,
but we provide the details in our replication package [14]. Nevertheless, defect detection during
code review is also influenced by other factors, such as code complexity, change size, and project
characteristics [23, 75]. Future work is necessary to incorporate these factors into the CCSR outcome
and achieve a more accurate assessment of how code reviewer recommendation systems influence
the risk of introducing new defects.
   Internal Validity. Although Monte Carlo simulations are computationally expensive, they
were initially considered necessary to examine the performance of reviewer recommenders [41].
However, Monte Carlo simulations are typically used when an event is sporadic [55], e.g., a banking
crisis, and require more processing power than we have available. The goal of our simulations is to
directly compare the effectiveness of recommenders in mitigating the risk of inducing defects when
developers integrate a change into the codebase. In our context, code reviews happen every day and
are not a rare event, e.g., over 58k reviews across all projects in our data sample. When we replace
one of the actual reviewers with the top candidate in simulations, each recommender replaces the
same reviewer. By consistently choosing the same seeded random reviewer, the variation in outcome
measures will only come from different recommenders, making it easier to interpret the outcomes.
This seeded random approach achieves our goal of evaluating different recommenders and explicitly
does not evaluate how randomly selected reviewers would have impacted the outcome measures.
In this study, we examine the performance of reviewer recommenders across 58k reviews from
three open-source projects, making it unlikely that this random selection will lead to systematic
bias. Future work could run thousands of Monte Carlo simulations to understand the variations in
outcome measures.
   The goal of this study is to mitigate the risk of new defects rather than to predict them. As a
result, our approach is less susceptible to the well-known limitations of SZZ [60] because we do
not aim to improve defect prediction accuracy but to reduce the potential harm of defect-prone
code changes. In this context, the exact accuracy of SZZ is less critical because our method does
not rely on precise defect labeling. Instead, it shifts the distribution of review expertise toward
changes that are more likely to induce defects. Although SZZ is not perfectly accurate, it provides
a reasonable signal of defect risk. This signal enables us to focus reviewer expertise on higher-risk
changes, unlike traditional recommenders that lack any notion of defect risk. In this study, we
rely on an existing dataset to get the likelihood of defect induction [28]. Naturally, more accurate
defect risk models would allow for even better targeting of reviewer expertise. However, given that
SZZ is not entirely inaccurate, it establishes a practical lower bound on the safety improvements
achievable through code reviewer recommendations. We are excited to see the future work of other
researchers who tune highly accurate defect risk models.
   Generalizability. We select large and long-lived open-source software projects led by an industry
or a community. As noted in prior works, reviewer recommendations are not necessary for small
projects because there are few potential candidates, and they are obvious to the development
team [35, 41]. In this work, we assume that additional reviewers would be available in software
projects. This assumption allows the AddExpertRec(𝐷𝑡 ) approach to add an extra expert reviewer
to defect-prone pull requests. However, smaller software projects may lack sufficient resources to
assign extra reviewers, limiting the feasibility of recommenders like AddExpertRec(25) that suggest

                                   Proc. ACM Softw. Eng., Vol. 3, No. FSE, Article FSE008. Publication date: July 2026.
FSE008:16                                                               Mohammadali Sefidi Esfahani and Peter C. Rigby


an additional reviewer for a higher number of pull requests. Future work is necessary to validate
our results in other development contexts, like proprietary projects.
   In addition to resource constraints, the code reviewer recommenders may suggest reviewers
who have stopped contributing to the project, i.e. stale recommendation [29]. While working on
SofiaWL’s replication package [21], we observe that it excludes developers who become inactive
in later periods from the pool of potential developers and prevents recommenders from making
stale recommendations. However, how long the developer will stay with the project is unknown
and is an inappropriate use of future information. In this study, we correct this methodological
issue, but do not explicitly address the problem of stale recommendations. Although predicting
developer departures and mitigating stale recommendations are beyond the scope of this paper,
handling stale suggestions and other forms of temporary unavailability, such as vacation periods,
represents a promising direction for future research.
   Replication and Reproducibility. Some of the existing recommenders in the literature, such as
cHRev [78] and WhoDo [4], do not provide official replication packages or source code. Therefore,
we extend the official replication package of SofiaWL [21], which implements the existing code
reviewer recommender from the literature. We integrate our proposed strategies into this package to
conduct simulations and evaluate their impact on outcome measures. In our replication package, we
simulate the effect of more recommenders on the risk of new defects, including AuthorshipRec [8],
RevOwnRec [64], RetentionRec [41], and LearnRec [41]. However, we only report the results of
prior state-of-the-art recommenders in the body of the paper for clarity purposes. This replication
package [14] is also available for future researchers who wish to replicate or extend our results.

7     Literature
We position our findings in the research literature and discuss how we advance our understanding
of reviewer recommendations and their impact on the risk of introducing new defects during code
review.

7.1    Understanding Code Review Practice
Fagan [15] introduced software inspection in 1976 by conducting a detailed experiment that
conclusively demonstrated its effectiveness in detecting defects early in the design process. He also
highlighted that design artifacts that were not reviewed would lead to defects slipping through
to later stages, thereby increasing the overall effort of software development. Over the years,
researchers have provided evidence of code inspection’s benefits, especially in terms of defect
finding, but the cumbersome, time-consuming, and synchronous nature of this software inspection
hinders its universal adoption in practice [5]. Current code review practices favor a lightweight,
tool-based process known as Modern Code Review, which focuses on reviewing code changes
through asynchronous discussions among reviewers [5, 58]. In this study, we examine the effect of
code reviewer recommendations on defect detection during the code review process.

7.2    Defect Detection During Code Review
During software development and maintenance, developers modify the source code to add new
features and enhance the performance, but these modifications may introduce more defects [16].
Defects diminish the software quality [42] and reputation [49], and increase the maintenance
cost [68]. Prior work [28] has demonstrated that through code review, engineering managers can
naturally reduce the risk of inducing new defects and mitigate the adverse consequences of defects
by assigning expert reviewers to defect-prone code changes. In that work, Kazemi et al. quantify the
impact of reviewer recommenders on the risk of inducing new defects by randomly replacing one
of the reviewers with the top recommended candidate. At the replacement level, our analysis shows

that the expertise-based reviewer recommenders can effectively reduce the risk of introducing new
defects, but fail to spread the knowledge at risk to turnover among the development team. In this
study, we aim to spread knowledge among the development team and mitigate the risk of defects
more effectively by adding an extra expert reviewer to defect-prone patches, instead of replacing
existing reviewers.

7.3   Code Reviewer Recommenders
Identifying the right reviewers for a given code change is a challenging and critical step in the code
review process [5, 6, 15, 19, 78]. Inappropriate selection of reviewers can slow down the review
process [66] or lower its quality [5, 9]. The research on code reviewer recommenders focuses
on the problem of automatically assigning review requests to developers who are most likely to
provide expert feedback [6, 11]. Prior works leverage heuristic-based scoring functions [4, 20, 22, 28,
32, 41, 51, 66, 78], genetic search algorithms [2, 10, 46, 52], matrix factorization and collaborative
filtering [11, 61, 73], machine learning [3, 26, 47, 62], text mining [36, 37, 39, 72], and multiplex
relationship graphs [38, 50, 56, 74, 76, 77, 80] to develop advanced reviewer recommendation
systems.
   Traditionally, researchers evaluate code reviewer recommenders by applying them to historical
data and comparing recommended reviewer lists to actual reviewers who performed the review [29].
This evaluation pipeline is based on the assumption that actual reviewers are the ideal candidates
to review code changes. However, Kovalenko et al. [35] argue that developers often know the top
recommendations and focusing on metrics like Top-K Accuracy and MRR rarely provides additional
value for the development team. Moreover, prior work [18] found that recommended reviewers
who did not perform the review would often have been appropriate assignees. These arguments
suggest considering alternative perspectives and evaluating outcomes beyond merely predicting
the actual reviewers.
   Recent works expand the goals of code reviewer recommenders to include mitigating knowledge
loss from turnover and avoiding overburdening the development team. Mirsaeedi and Rigby [41]
evaluate review recommenders in the context of ensuring expertise during the review process,
Expertise outcome, reducing the reviewer workload of the core team, CoreWorkload outcome, and
reducing the number of files at risk to turnover, FaR outcome. Moreover, Asthana et al. [4] examine
the performance of WhoDo recommender based on the average number of open reviews, average PR
completion time, and average number of reviews per PR.
   The historical analysis [20] reveals that the top 20% of reviewers do between 75% and 84% of the
work in open-source projects. However, prior outcomes fail to capture the workload distribution
across reviewers. Hajari et al. [20] adapt the Gini Coefficient and Lorenz curve from economics
and introduce the GiniWorkload of reviewers to measure workload distribution across the team.
In this study, we suggest an extra reviewer for defect-prone pull requests to further mitigate the
risk of defects during the code review process. For example, the AddExpertRec(75) recommender
adds an extra reviewer to 6.1% and 12.7% of pull requests in Rust and Kubernetes projects, but
the GiniWorkload of reviewers is reduced by -0.3% and -0.4%. This is because the GiniWorkload
outcome examines the distribution of work among the development team rather than focusing on
the number of code reviews. When AddExpertRec(75) adds reviewers to defect-prone PRs in these
projects, more developers from the team participate in the review process, and the work distributes
better. Although the Rev++ outcome clearly presents an increase in the number of code reviews,
future work is required to investigate other outcomes like the average active load [4] and average
number of reviews [33, 54] for each reviewer.
   To quantify the impact of reviewer recommenders on the risk of inducing defects after code
integration, Kazemi et al. [28] have proposed the Changeset Safety Ratio (CSR) outcome. However,

the CSR outcome fails to account for the knowledge of the entire reviewer team and the author’s
level of expertise. In this work, we introduce the Contribution-aware Changeset Safety Ratio (CCSR)
outcome, which measures the risk of inducing new defects during code merges based on aggregated
knowledge of reviewers and the author’s level of expertise. In our simulations, we calculate the
Expertise, GiniWorkload, KRT , CCSR, and Rev++ outcomes to provide a complete evaluation of our
recommenders. Since our novel approach suggests an additional reviewer for defect-prone pull
requests, the changes in CCSR and Rev++ outcomes, and their trade-off with the KRT outcome, are the
most important measures in our work. The simulation results show that our novel AddExpertRec(𝐷𝑡 )
strategy increases the CCSR outcome from an average of 18.8% to 30.8% and reduces the KRT
outcome from an average of -22.1% to -63.1%, depending on the proportion of extra reviews varying
from 8.1% to 59.6%.

8 Concluding Remarks
In this work, we aggregate the knowledge of all developers involved in a PR to create the accurate
Contribution-aware Changeset Safety Ratio (CCSR). We also suggest an extra expert reviewer for
defect-prone pull requests to reduce the likelihood of new defects more effectively when a developer
integrates a change into the codebase. We evaluate the tradeoffs of a diverse set of outcomes.
   The following are highlights and learning from our empirical results and simulations:

    • The top expert reviewer does not represent the aggregated knowledge of the entire reviewer
       team, and the prior work CSR outcome [28] overestimates the top reviewer’s defect-finding
       effectiveness. We introduce CCSR, which accurately captures the knowledge of the engineers
       involved in the review and the defect risk.
    • Reviewer recommenders that suggest expert developers for code changes are the most
       effective in reducing the risk of inducing defects. AcHRev and cHRev achieves the ΔCCSR of
      19.6% and 18.8% across projects. Despite this effectiveness, they substantially increase KRT
       by an average of 50% and 28%, respectively.
    • It is essential to examine the performance of code reviewer recommenders from diverse
       perspectives. The hybrid recommenders that trade off between knowledge distribution and
       defect finding effectiveness, like RAR, can balance the outcome measures simultaneously. The
       RAR recommender enhances the Expertise, KRT , and CCSR outcomes by an average of 13%,
      -5%, and 17.7% with the cost of slightly increasing the average GiniWorkload of reviewers by
       0.2%.
    • Assigning an additional expert reviewer to defect-prone code changes can both prevent new
       bugs and mitigate the risk of developer turnover. The AddExpertRec(75) is able to maintain
       the same defect finding effectiveness as AcHRev but drastically reduces the average ΔKRT
       from 50% to -22% with the cost of adding a reviewer to only 8% of PRs.

   These findings highlight the potential of suggesting additional expert reviewers for defect-
prone pull requests to prevent new defects after code integration and substantially mitigate the
risk of knowledge loss from developer turnover. Ultimately, it is up to managers to determine
which approach best suits their specific situation, taking into account project priorities, resource
constraints, and available budgets.
