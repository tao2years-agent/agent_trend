                                                                      Abstract
         We examine the impacts of generative AI on productivity, output, and employment in software
         engineering. Firm production requires combining complementary inputs, across which AI has
         differential productivity effects. Inputs that are difficult to automate can become “bottlenecks,”
         constraining output and shaping labor demand. We study these dynamics using a novel proprietary
         dataset from an engineering analytics platform, covering 445M work events — GitHub coding
         activity, Jira issues, and Google Calendar events — for 720,542 workers at 655 firms. We estimate
         the effects of two generations of AI coding tools — assistants and agents — by exploiting variation
         in timing of firm adoption. AI substantially increases code output but has small effects on final
         software output and no detectable effect on employment. Code review becomes the bottleneck.
         Code waits longer to reach a reviewer, spends longer in active review, and receives more requested
         changes. We build and calibrate a model of firm production and AI adoption, then use it to simulate
         AI’s long-run impacts under continued firm adjustment and technological progress, predicting
         growth in output and declines in total employment, with smaller effects if the review bottleneck
         persists.

1    Introduction
Artificial intelligence (AI) has advanced rapidly in recent years, prompting significant excitement as
well as concern about its labor market consequences (Kwa et al., 2025; Epoch AI, 2026; Sajadieh et al.,
2026). Yet there remains substantial uncertainty over the extent and trajectory of this transformation.
This new technology has the potential to deliver significant economic growth and generate widespread
job displacement. However, its impacts will depend not only on its technical capabilities, but also
on how it is integrated into existing production and market structures (Davidson, 2021; Briggs &
Kodnani, 2023; Chui et al., 2023; Autor, 2024; Acemoglu, 2024; Korinek et al., 2026).
    We study the impacts of AI on productivity, output, and employment in software engineering.
In particular, we focus on how the structure of firm production shapes these effects. Initial indicators
of the technology’s impacts have demonstrated its promise, with numerous studies documenting
that AI raises productivity on specific tasks across domains (e.g., Noy & Zhang, 2023; Peng et al.,
2023; Brynjolfsson, Li, & Raymond, 2025). However, these productivity gains may not translate into
commensurate changes in firms’ final output or employment. Production processes generally combine
complementary inputs, some of which are more difficult to automate than others. These inputs can
thus become “bottlenecks”, limiting the pass-through of productivity gains to final output and leading
firms to reallocate labor (Kremer, 1993; Aghion et al., 2017; Brynjolfsson et al., 2018; Acemoglu &
Restrepo, 2019; Gans & Goldfarb, 2026; C. Jones & Tonetti, 2026). However, empirically identifying
where bottlenecks emerge — and how these bottlenecks shape firms’ output and employment — is
difficult, as doing so requires highly granular data on internal firm production processes.
    We leverage a novel proprietary dataset from Jellyfish (JF), an analytics platform that companies
use to understand the activity of their software engineering teams. Our data allows us to measure
details of the software production process, covering 445 million work events of 720,542 workers across
655 firms, from January 2021 to August 2026. JF combines data from industry-standard software
engineering tools, and we use four of these tools: (1) code version control systems, such as GitHub,
which track engineers’ code; (2) issue management systems, such as Jira, which measure engineers’
progress on and completion of software tasks and projects; (3) scheduling tools, such as Google
Calendar, which capture meeting activity and time allocation; and (4) generative AI usage at the firm
and worker level, for GitHub Copilot, Cursor, and Claude Code. Thus, whereas existing papers have
studied individual outcomes in isolation — e.g., AI’s impacts on productivity or on employment — to
our knowledge, our paper is the first to examine AI’s productivity, output, and employment effects
within a single end-to-end view of production.
    Software has been at the frontier of advances both in AI’s technical capabilities and in organiza-
tional adaptation: AI labs have invested significant resources in training models to complete coding

tasks, such that tools have become highly competent, and adoption has been widespread (Kinder
et al., 2024; Eloundou et al., 2024). For instance, the frontier AI labs state that AI is already writing
80% of their code, and they are reorganizing workflows in response (The Anthropic Institute, 2026).
Given the rapid advances in these tools’ capabilities, we examine the impacts of two generations of
AI coding tools — AI coding assistants and AI coding agents. First released in late 2022, AI coding
assistants provide workers with localized code edit suggestions. Advances in AI model capabilities
led to the release of agentic coding tools in late 2024, which have the capacity to semi-autonomously
complete tasks of greater length and complexity.
    To structure our empirical analysis, we begin by building a model of firm production and AI
adoption, in which production requires complementary inputs and AI has differential productivity
effects across inputs. Specifically, software development consists of two stages, to which the firm
allocates labor inputs: code writing and code review. AI adoption affects production through two
channels. First, it increases the productivity of coders, increasing the volume of code requiring review.
Second, it decreases the quality of code entering review, increasing the review effort required per
unit of code and decreasing review productivity. Both channels can generate a “bottleneck” in the
review stage, limiting the pass-through of the coding productivity shock to final output and shaping
firms’ labor allocation towards the bottleneck task. Although we describe our model in the context
of software development, its ideas are generalizable to a broad range of settings in which production
requires complementary inputs that are differentially affected by AI. In particular, the structure of
“implementation” versus “management” or “review” is nearly ubiquitous across knowledge work
settings — and our model demonstrates that even if AI adoption increases the speed of implementation,
output will be limited by the speed of management or review, and AI can even create spillovers that
further slow down these surrounding processes.
    We next test the predictions of our model on the short- to medium-run impacts of AI empirically.
We estimate the effects of AI adoption using a staggered difference-in-differences design, exploiting
variation in the timing of firms’ adoption of AI coding assistants and agents. Our identification
strategy relies on the assumption that absent adoption, earlier- and later-adopting firms would have
followed similar trajectories in outcomes. In practice, the technology procurement process typically
involves a number of steps — including information gathering, security and legal approval, and small-
scale piloting (Rapoport et al., 2025; IBM, 2024). The lengths of these processes differ significantly
across firms, in a way that is plausibly orthogonal to firms’ growth trajectories, and we leverage this
variation for our identification strategy.
    With this design, we begin by studying the effects of generative AI tools on measures of interme-
diate code output, using three outcomes: lines of code added; commits, which represent incremental
saved changes to code; and pull requests, which represent larger code submissions for review. AI



                                                   3
assistants lead to moderate increases in code output — 12 months after adoption, firms exhibit an
increase of 30.4% in lines of code, 16.8% in commits, and 20.9% in pull requests. AI agents lead to
larger additional increases in all three measures — 44.0% for lines of code, 36.0% for commits, and
35.7% for pull requests opened. These estimates are consistent with recent work on the effects of
generative AI tools on coding productivity (Cui et al., 2024; Hoffmann et al., 2024; Song et al., 2024;
Sarkar, 2025; Demirer, Musolff, & Yang, 2026).
    Does the extra code written translate into increases in firm-level software output? In principle,
extra code may not generate new useful output for the firm: code may be subject to errors; and even
if it is not subject to errors, surrounding production steps may constrain its deployment. We use two
measures of output: resolution of Jira issues, which occur once a body of coding work is deployed to
production; and resolution of Jira epics, which represent completion of larger projects or features.
The effects from AI assistants are near zero, at −1.5% on issues and 3.4% on epics. The effects from AI
agents are slightly larger — 13.7% on issues and 18.0% on epics — with the effect of agents on issues
resolved being borderline significant. One concern is that AI adoption may lead to an increase in the
size or complexity of issues, such that the raw issue count would understate true output growth. We
address this by analyzing the text descriptions of Jira issues, using machine learning tools to predict
the length of time it would take for an engineer to resolve them. We use two methods — first, using
an LLM to label issues with their assessed length; and second, using a supervised learning method
to predict the outcome label of time to resolution. We find no evidence of an effect on the average
predicted length of each issue. In turn, we observe effects on final software output that are much
smaller in magnitude than those of the intermediate code output.
    If firms do not adjust on the margin of increased output, they may adjust by cutting employment.
Many companies have stated that AI tools are already affecting demand for software engineers, with
some firms announcing layoffs due to AI (e.g., Kessler, 2023; Roose, 2025), and others announcing
increases in hiring (e.g., Morgan Stanley, 2025). We assess the effect on engineering employment,
measured by the number of active workers at each firm on Jellyfish. We cannot attribute significant
employment changes to AI, though our estimates are noisy. With AI assistants, we estimate an effect
of 3.0%, ruling out changes below −4.7%. For AI agents, we estimate an effect of −2.6%, ruling out
changes below −15.6%.
    Taken together, our results show that while AI tools substantially raise measures of intermediate
code output, these gains do not translate into proportional changes in software output or employment.
How can we reconcile these findings?
    We turn to examining the structure of software production. Writing code is only one step in
a multi-stage production process: upstream, managers must plan and scope features; downstream,
written code must be reviewed and tested before it can be deployed to production. We impute time



                                                  4
usage from the Jellyfish data and validate this imputation with a survey of 100 Prolific engineers
and external survey estimates of time allocation, finding that engineers spend 30–40% of their time
writing code; the remainder goes to these complementary tasks. When AI raises productivity in code
writing, complementary steps may serve as bottlenecks. To provide color to our results, we perform
qualitative interviews with 21 workers at software companies. Anecdotally, engineers have pointed
to these surrounding tasks as important bottlenecks that limit the gains from AI:
      People could make a huge number of commits quickly, but code review was still the bottleneck. On
      one project, only one senior engineer was allowed to approve changes because he was the expert on
      the codebase. He was so bandwidth constrained that he could only review my code once a week. After
      some outages that were caused by AI-generated commits, there were also new rules requiring senior
      engineer approval for AI-generated code, which created even more review bottlenecks.

Our data provides us with particularly granular insights into the code review process, which we
examine as one source of a bottleneck. Though firms do not typically hire separate code writers
and code reviewers, workers are also not perfectly substitutable across these stages. In particular,
as evidenced by the description above, workers typically only review code that they are familiar
with (82.8% of reviews, compared to 17.2% if reviewers were assigned at random); additionally, code
written by a senior engineer is more often reviewed by another senior engineer (75.7% of reviews for
senior coders, compared to 32.8% for junior coders).
    We find evidence of a bottleneck in this stage: after adoption of AI agents, the average time from
the code’s review submission to its merge increases by 56.1%. This change represents both an increase
in the time that code waits in a “backlog,” by 59.9%, and an increase in the time after code has begun
active review, by 58.5%. The latter is consistent with a decline in review productivity, i.e., an increase
in the review intensity. The share of code changes that receive at least one review comment increases
by 13.1%, and the number of comments on each code change increases by 38.6%. For AI assistants,
we do not observe significant effects on any of these margins, which we attribute to the fact that
the effect on the amount of code written is comparatively small in magnitude. This bottleneck is
not eliminated by adoption of AI tools in the review process — 79.1% of firms have adopted AI code
review tools, but still largely keep humans in the loop, with the overwhelming majority of reviews
and comments being written by humans.
    Finally, we bring our empirical estimates to our model of software production. First, we estimate
the model using minimum distance to recover effects that we cannot observe directly. We observe the
impacts of AI adoption on intermediate code output and on employment, but not on coding or review
productivity — i.e., the rate at which labor inputs produce code and reviews — or on how workers
change their time allocation between coding and review. AI agents raise coding productivity by 73.7%
and slightly lower review productivity (−7.0%), and firms shift labor from coding to review: coding
labor changes by −22.7% and review labor by 43.4%. Notably, the effect on coding productivity is


                                                      5
larger than the effect on code output, because the increase in final output combined with the decline
in review productivity implies that some labor is reallocated away from coding towards review.
    Second, with the estimated model, we simulate the potential future impacts of AI under two
types of scenarios: continued firm adjustment and continued technological advancement. The simu-
lations help to highlight the importance of three factors in shaping the longer-run impacts of AI on
economic outcomes: the trajectory of technological advancement, the software product market, and
the persistence of the review bottleneck. First, the size of the software product market will govern
how much firms can expand output, and in turn how much they will cut employment. We begin by
characterizing a threshold for the product demand elasticity, above which an adopting firm sees a
net increase in employment and a larger increase in output, and below which employment falls. We
obtain a threshold of 𝜀 = 3.68. Existing estimates of demand elasticities for software products range
from 1.50 to 3.73 (Ghose & Han, 2014; Derdenger, 2014; Ghose & Sundararajan, 2005), nearly all below
this threshold. At the mean of these estimates, 𝜀 = 2.27, our model predicts an increase in output of
16.1% and a decline in employment of 13.2%. Next, we combine our estimates with forecasts of the
trajectory of technological advancement to simulate possible growth and employment trajectories
over the next five years. In particular, the simulations highlight the importance of whether review
remains a bottleneck — i.e., whether AI raises review productivity as quickly as coding productivity. If
review productivity stays fixed, the model predicts that output rises by 57.8% by 2031 and employment
falls by 10.3%, with output leveling off once the review constraint binds. If review productivity keeps
pace, output rises by 341.4% and employment falls by 76.2%.

Related literature. Our project contributes to several literatures. First, a long literature examines
the impacts of new technologies on labor market outcomes. Most recently, this literature has turned
to the effects of AI. These papers document widespread adoption of AI (Chatterji et al., 2025, 2026;
Yotzov et al., 2026), as well as productivity gains across settings (Choi & Schwarcz, 2023; Dell’Acqua
et al., 2023; Chen & Chan, 2024; Kim et al., 2024; Noy & Zhang, 2023; Otis et al., 2024; Roldan-Mones,
2024; Brynjolfsson, Li, & Raymond, 2025; Dell’Acqua et al., 2025; Dillon et al., 2025; Manzoor et al.,
2025). Several papers also examine the effects on firm output and economic growth. At the firm level,
AI adoption has been linked to growth in sales, productivity, and market value (Babina et al., 2024;
Czarnitzki et al., 2023; Eisfeldt et al., 2023); at the aggregate level, a growing literature models its
potential effects on economic growth, with predictions ranging from modest productivity gains to
explosive growth (Aghion et al., 2017; Nordhaus, 2021; Trammell & Korinek, 2023; Erdil & Besiroglu,
2023; Acemoglu, 2024; Besiroglu et al., 2024; Filippucci et al., 2024; C. I. Jones, 2024; Korinek & Suh,
2024; C. Jones, 2026; Davidson et al., 2026; Korinek et al., 2026). Finally, several papers examine
AI’s employment effects, with some finding employment changes, particularly losses for entry-level



                                                   6
workers (Brynjolfsson, Chandar, & Chen, 2025; de Souza, 2025; Klein Teeselink, 2025; Lichtinger &
Hosseini Maasoum, 2025), and others finding no employment effect (Humlum & Vestergaard, 2025;
Gimbel et al., 2025; Iscenko & Millet, 2026). We contribute to this literature in two ways. First, we
estimate the effects of firm-level adoption of AI tools, which allows us to capture the aggregate impacts
of AI, accounting for incomplete take-up and heterogeneous utility across workers. Furthermore, it
allows us to estimate AI’s productivity effect, and connect it to changes in firm output, employment,
and firm organization. Second, we provide estimates of these effects across generations of AI coding
tools, then use those to simulate future impacts, allowing us to examine how the impacts of AI evolve
as the technology improves.
    Second, a broader literature examines how productivity gains — including from new technologies
— translate to changes in firm output and employment. Although the empirical literature on AI’s
effects on output and employment is growing, it is often less clear why productivity gains do —
or do not — pass through to these downstream outcomes. A large theoretical literature provides
structure to understand this pass-through. While new technologies can create productivity gains in
individual tasks, complementary tasks can serve as “bottlenecks,” constraining changes in output
and shaping the reallocation of labor (e.g., Kremer, 1993; B. Jones, 2025; Demirer, Horton, et al., 2026;
Gans & Goldfarb, 2026; C. Jones & Tonetti, 2026). Relatedly, task-based models characterize how new
technologies reshape the allocation of labor across tasks, determining whether they augment labor,
substitute for labor, or create new work (e.g., Acemoglu & Restrepo, 2018; Acemoglu, 2024). Finally, a
large related literature in operations and product development characterizes how production systems
respond when one stage speeds up (Thomke, 1998, 2003), including how queues and waiting times
grow (Little, 1961; Kingman, 1961; Little, 2011), how lower-quality output and rework may slow
production (So & Tang, 1995; Smith & Eppinger, 1997a,b; Loch & Terwiesch, 1998), and how firms
can reallocate capacity in response (Jordan & Graves, 1995; Zychlinski & Gurvich, 2026). Limitations
in detailed micro-data make it difficult to directly test the empirical importance of these theoretical
mechanisms. Our paper contributes to this literature by providing direct empirical evidence on the
importance of these channels.
    Third, a related literature examines how organizations adapt to technology adoption. This
work argues that realizing the benefits of new technologies requires complementary organizational
investments — including changes to workflows, incentive structures, and other managerial practices
— rather than technological adoption alone (e.g., Bresnahan et al., 2002; Aral et al., 2012; Brynjolfsson
et al., 2018; Seamans & Raj, 2019; Raj & Seamans, 2019; Dixon et al., 2021; Anthony, 2021; Berg et al.,
2023; Anthony et al., 2023; Agrawal et al., 2024; Beane & Anthony, 2024). Relatedly, a management and
marketing literature examines how firms organize the process of transforming ideas into successful
products, emphasizing the integrated roles of marketing capabilities, product architecture, and



                                                    7
organizational structure (e.g., Henderson & Clark, 1990; Griffin & Hauser, 1996; Baldwin & Clark,
2000; Hauser et al., 2006; MacCormack et al., 2006; Mu, 2015; Kyriakopoulos et al., 2015). More recently,
this literature has begun examining how generative AI reshapes marketing content and competition
in product markets (Guha et al., 2021; Cillo & Rubera, 2025; Exner et al., 2025; Goldberg & Lam,
2025). We contribute to these literatures by providing insights into the effects of AI on the product
development process in software engineering, including how firms should respond organizationally.
    Finally, within these literatures, several papers study the impacts of AI on software engineering.
Studies generally find widespread adoption of AI coding tools across occupations and tasks (Johnston
et al., 2026). Many have also found that these tools improve worker productivity, measured by coding
output and task completion time (Peng et al., 2023; Cui et al., 2024; Song et al., 2024) — though
some report negative effects (Becker et al., 2025). A subset of studies have examined the impacts on
work patterns, demonstrating shifts in individual work activity and AI usage patterns (Hoffmann et
al., 2024; Yeverechyahu et al., 2024; Sarkar, 2025; Sarkar & Melas-Kyriazi, 2026). The most closely
related paper is Demirer, Musolff, & Yang (2026). Using data on open-source coding repositories,
Demirer, Musolff, & Yang (2026) show that GitHub Copilot adoption substantially increases lines
of code written and commits, but has much smaller effects on pull requests and product releases.
Demirer, Musolff, & Yang (2026) attribute this incomplete pass-through to human bottlenecks in
the production process. Relative to this paper, our main contribution is to examine the effects of AI
adoption in a firm setting — with many of the firms in our sample producing enterprise software
— in which features of the production process and organizational structure may shape effects. Our
setting allows us to more directly identify the nature of bottlenecks — including explicitly using the
granularity of our data to document a bottleneck in the review stage, and to characterize the way
that firms reorganize in response.
    The rest of the paper proceeds as follows. In Section 2, we describe our data and institutional
details about software engineering and AI, including details about the data classification and AI
take-up. In Section 3, we describe the model. In Section 4, we describe the effects of AI on productivity,
output, and employment. In Section 5, we provide evidence on production bottlenecks. In Section 6,
we estimate our model and simulate the future impacts of AI. In Section 7, we conclude.

2.1.1      Jellyfish

Our primary source of data is Jellyfish (“JF”), an analytics platform used by firms to understand the
activity of their software engineering teams. Firms use JF to monitor software activity and project
progress, and to understand engineers’ productivity, including in relation to their use of AI coding
tools. To provide these analytics, JF integrates data on engineers’ activity from several tools. We
make use of four main sources throughout our analysis.
        We analyze a sample of data from JF clients who have consented to their data being used for
research, between January 2021 and August 2026. There are 720,542 workers across 655 firms (Table
1). For each firm, Jellyfish is able to access data from a tool through the tool’s API integration, which
the firm must agree to using.
        Software production typically follows well-defined and documented processes (Pressman, 2010;
Atlassian, 2026). First, teams determine which software changes to implement — this process typically
involves integrating customer requests with internal product and engineering objectives. Second,
engineers implement these tasks by writing code. Third, teammates will review and test the proposed
code. This process typically involves tests that verify the correctness of the code and its integration
with the broader code base, as well as its functionality from the user’s perspective. Finally, once
approved, the code is deployed to production. Our data provides insights into each stage of this
process. Figure 1 visualizes this workflow and the data generated at each stage.

Issue tracking data. Software teams employ “issue tracking” systems to organize, assign, and
monitor engineering work.1 Work is typically organized hierarchically. Related work items are
grouped within “projects”, which often contain one or more “epics” representing large features or
initiatives. Epics are in turn decomposed into individual “issues,” which represent discrete units of
engineering work that can be assigned to engineers.
        Each issue can include a text description, links to related issues, and the issue category (e.g.,
“Task”, “Bug”, or “Service Request”). The text description of each issue is typically rich: in our analysis
sample, the median length of the description is 580 characters. We provide two examples of the Jira
interface in Appendix Figure A1. Panel A displays an example of a team’s Jira board, which provides
an overview of its issues, including their statuses. Panel B displays an example of an individual Jira
issue, including its associated fields.

reviewed, tested, and deployed, a team member marks the issue as “resolved” — i.e., complete. In

Code version control data. Software engineering teams employ “version control” systems to record,
organize, and coordinate changes to a shared codebase. Industry-standard version control systems
share a common structure.2
        Engineers write code on their local machines. As they update their code, they “commit” changes
to their branch to incrementally save progress. When work is ready to be incorporated into the
collective codebase, the engineer creates a “pull request”. This pull request is then reviewed, typically
by a manager or teammate. If needed, the reviewer may submit a request for changes and leave
comments detailing the needed changes. Then, the engineer and reviewer will iterate on these
changes. If the review process is successfully completed, the code is then incorporated into the shared
repository (“merged”); otherwise, the code is not incorporated into the shared repository.3 We provide
two examples of the interface for GitHub in Appendix Figure A2. Panel A displays the interface for
committing code. Panel B displays the interface for reviewing and commenting on a pull request.
        Version control systems are ubiquitous in software engineering teams: for instance, the world’s
largest version control platform, GitHub, is used by around 90% of Fortune 100 firms (GitHub, 2025).
JF integrates data from several version control platforms, including GitHub, GitLab, and Bitbucket.
        Our analysis sample includes 297,586,102 commits, for an average of 24.27 per worker-month.

of the engineer making the commit, and the text associated with the commit. We do not observe

time periods in which they are pushing more commits, but the frequency of the two updates is very
different: in our analysis sample, workers make 5.50 commits for every pull request.

Google Calendar data. For a subset of 192 firms, JF receives data from engineers’ Google Calendar
accounts. Google Calendar is a scheduling tool: users create calendar events and share them with one
another. Each calendar event includes information on the individuals who are invited, individuals
who accept versus decline, text description, and meeting length. In our analysis, we use the Google
Calendar data to assess relative time allocation between coding and non-coding work. Our analysis
sample covers 20,315,197 events, for an average of 6.59 per worker-month.

HR data. Firms may also input HR information into the JF platform. This information includes

2.3     Jira issue classification
Classification method. We measure final software output using the Jira data on issues and epics.
Our analysis of changes in final output relies on classification of Jira issues and epics by their size.
We assess the size of each Jira issue using two complementary methods.
      First, we use a large language model (LLM) to label a sample of tasks, then scale those labels to the
full dataset using a supervised learning approach. For the labeling step, we prompt the GPT-4o API
to classify each issue based on the estimated hours an experienced software engineer would require
to complete it, without the assistance of AI. The prompt provides the task summary, description,
issue type, parent task summary, and project name as context, and we label a 0.5% sample of issues,
stratified by company. To scale these labels to the full dataset, we use a supervised learning approach,
which predicts this LLM-label from the text description of the Jira issue. To do this, we first convert
each issue’s text description into numerical embeddings, then train a supervised learning model to
predict the GPT-assigned length label from the embeddings. Full details are provided in Appendix
Section F.2.
      Second, we construct a complementary measure of task size from Jira metadata using cycle
time — the number of days elapsed between a task being opened and resolved. We train the same
architecture described above to predict log cycle time from the embeddings, using issues resolved in
2022 as the training set (approximately 2 million issues with non-missing cycle time). We then apply
this trained model to the entire dataset of Jira issues, to predict the amount of time it would take to
resolve the issue.

Validation of supervised learning method. We validate our supervised learning approach in
Appendix Table A2. We take the GPT-assigned labels as the true labels and the outputs of the
supervised learning step as the predicted labels. The table displays a comparison of these labels for a
holdout sample of tasks. Because the length metric is continuous, we use the mean squared error
(MSE) as our main validation metric. We obtain an MSE of 0.410 (on a 0–3 scale), which indicates
that our model performs reasonably well.

2.4     AI coding tools
Background to AI coding tools. Finally, we describe AI coding tools. We refer to three primary
types of AI coding tools: AI coding assistants, AI coding agents, and AI code review tools. Appendix
Figure A3 displays examples of the visual interface observed by users when interacting with AI
coding tools.
      AI coding assistants are tools that generate real-time code suggestions within the software
application in which developers write and edit code. As a developer types, the assistant predicts and
autocompletes the next lines of code; developers can also describe a desired function in plain language
and have the assistant generate an implementation. AI coding assistants first became available in
business and enterprise license form in February 2023, with the release of GitHub Copilot’s business
and enterprise licenses. A wide range of AI coding assistants are now commercially available. We
focus on GitHub Copilot and Cursor, two of the most widely adopted coding assistants, for which
Jellyfish provides API integrations.
      AI coding agents are tools that can semi-autonomously complete longer, multi-step tasks. Agents
can accept a task description and semi-independently plan and execute a sequence of actions. AI
coding agents first became available in November 2024, with the release of Cursor’s AI agent tool. A
wide range of AI coding agents are now commercially available; Claude Code is among the most
widely adopted. Although AI coding assistants and agents differ in terms of their interfaces, agents’
release largely represents advances in model capabilities that allow the tools to complete more
complex tasks. In turn, we conceptualize these tools as providing similar types of shocks to firms,
differing only in magnitude.
      Finally, AI tools are also able to perform code reviews, i.e., analyze pull requests. They can read
over code to flag potential bugs, security vulnerabilities, or other changes needed in the code, and
help to generate comments.
      All of these tools can generally be configured to use models from any of the leading AI laboratories,
including OpenAI (GPT series), Anthropic (Claude series), and Google (Gemini series). Generally,
new versions of AI models are available in each tool nearly immediately at release.

Adoption process. The firm-level technology procurement process typically involves several stages.
It is often initiated by senior management. The implementation is generally led by the IT team,
which manages enterprise technology usage. The IT team generally begins by evaluating different
possible technology vendors — e.g., comparing GitHub Copilot with Cursor. Then, the team must
get internal business approval and budget allocation. In larger organizations, business teams may
also negotiate pricing and contract terms with the technology provider. The tool must then receive
approval from legal and security teams, which assess issues related to data handling and privacy,

intellectual property ownership, and compliance. Finally, some firms conduct a pilot program in
which a small subset of engineers tests the tool before broader deployment (Rapoport et al., 2025;
IBM, 2024).
    We gather information on the procurement processes across different firms through qualitative
interviews, and provide some illustrative examples in Appendix Section C. Two examples demonstrate
the contrast between firms in the length of the process. In one firm, the Head of AI Analytics and
Insights describes a structured process:

3     Model
We begin by developing a model of firm production and AI adoption. The model formalizes the
relationship between a technology-driven productivity shock, firm output, and labor demand. It
most directly builds on two literatures: first, a literature on the effects of AI adoption, based on the
importance of “bottlenecks” or “weak links” arising from complementarities between tasks (e.g.,
B. Jones, 2025; C. Jones & Tonetti, 2026); second, a literature from operations that characterizes how
production systems respond when one stage speeds up, but another is unable to keep pace, or rework
is needed (e.g., Little, 1961; Kingman, 1961; So & Tang, 1995).
     In our model, production involves two inputs that are perfect complements: coding and review.
Though AI adoption can increase productivity in coding, review can serve as a bottleneck if firms
are unable to adjust inputs fully, and because adjustment inherently shifts labor away from coding.
Furthermore, AI adoption can decrease the quality of code generated, decreasing review productivity
and worsening the bottleneck. Both of these channels limit AI’s effects on overall firm output and



                                                  17
shape its effects on labor demand.
      Although we write the model in terms of software production, the concepts apply to a range
of production settings in which a firm uses multiple complementary inputs that are differentially
affected by AI, and for which AI may change the quality of an intermediate output. In particular,
the setup of “implementation” versus “management” or “review” is ubiquitous across work domains.
Though AI tools can speed up implementation processes, output is still limited by the speed of
decision-making concerning what projects to pursue, or by the speed of verification; and the usage
of AI tools can make these surrounding tasks more difficult as well.
      Empirically, we estimate effects of two generations of AI tools — AI coding assistants and
AI coding agents. Though some researchers have conceptualized assistants and agents as distinct
technologies (e.g., Sarkar, 2025), we will conceive of them as creating different-sized shocks to coding
productivity and the bug rate. In other words, we abstract away from differences in the cognitive
experience associated with interacting with these iterations of tools, which have slight differences
in interfaces. We view this as a reasonable simplification, since agentic tools arose largely from
improvements in underlying model capabilities rather than from a new technology (e.g., Kwa et al.,
2025).

passes through to output. Second, the labor allocation is shaped by the relative productivities 𝐴𝐶 /𝐴𝑅 :
e.g., a firm whose coders are more productive, whose reviewers are relatively less productive, or
whose code is buggier, will allocate more labor towards review; and vice versa.

3.5     Testable implications
The model yields several testable implications for the short- to medium-run impacts of AI adoption.
In Sections 4 and 5, we provide reduced form evidence on these outcomes.
      1. Review ceiling. Senior review capacity caps how far output can grow. We measure this ceiling
         as the share of engineers who are senior divided by the share of code that requires senior
         review.
      2. Coding productivity. If AI raises coding productivity, code output should rise. We measure
         code output as lines of code added, commits, and pull requests opened.
      3. Final output and labor adjustment. If AI raises coding productivity, and any labor has been
         reallocated to review, then final software output will rise. We measure final software output as
         the number of Jira issues resolved, indicating deployment of the associated code to production;
         and the number of Jira epics resolved, indicating completion of larger software projects.
      4. Bug rate. If AI raises the bug rate, then code will spend longer in review. We measure the bug
         rate as the share of pull requests that receive a comment or are blocked from being merged.
        We measure the length of review as the time between the first comment on a pull request and
         the time the pull request is merged.
      5. Backlog and wait times. If AI affects either coding productivity or the bug rate, the review
         backlog and wait times should grow. They keep growing unless the firm optimally reallocates
         labor toward review. We measure the wait time as the time from the pull request being opened
         to the first comment. We measure the backlog size as the number of pull requests that are


                                                   26
        unreviewed (i.e., unmerged and have not received a comment).

Then, in Section 6, we estimate the model and use it to provide evidence on the projected long-run
effects of AI adoption, per Lemma 3 and Proposition 5.

4.1     Descriptive evidence
Before turning to causal estimates, we describe the raw data on the paper’s main outcomes.
      Appendix Figure A4 plots the distribution of code output across firms in three groups: adopters
before adoption, adopters after adoption, and firms that never adopt. We measure code output in
three ways: (1) lines of code added; (2) GitHub commits, which are incremental updates to code;
and (3) GitHub pull requests created, which are completed drafts of code submitted for review. We
display the firm-level distributions of these outcomes, normalized by the number of workers in the
firm, pre-AI adoption, post-AI adoption, and among non-adopters.
      Appendix Figure A5 shows the same distributions for our two measures of final software output.
As described in Section 2, software engineering teams use Jira to track work, which gives two natural
measures of output. (1) The number of Jira issues resolved per worker per month. Issues are discrete
units of engineering work — such as a bug fix or feature update — and are marked as complete when
the associated code is deployed to production. (2) The number of Jira epics resolved per worker per
month. Epics are larger software projects or features — i.e., groups of software tasks. Appendix Figure
A6 shows the distributions for two measures of code review: the number of days from when a pull
request is opened to when it is merged, and the number of comments per pull request.
      These distributions preview the paper’s main findings. Code output per worker is clearly higher
after adoption. For lines of code added, the median firm writes 3,115 lines per worker per month
before adopting AI assistants and 4,424 after, a 42% increase. The gap is larger for AI agents: the
median rises from 3,679 to 6,482 lines, or 76%. Commits and pull requests show the same pattern
on a smaller scale. The median number of commits rises 14% after AI assistant adoption (26.47 to
30.15) and 23% after agent adoption (27.94 to 34.45). The median number of pull requests created
rises 15% (4.84 to 5.59) and 32% (4.97 to 6.55). Each of these outcomes has a long right tail, and the
tail also shifts out after adoption: for lines of code, the 90th percentile rises from 10,609 to 14,753 for
AI assistants and from 14,750 to 18,580 for AI agents.



                                                    27
       Final software output also rises, but by less. For Jira issues resolved, the median rises 9% after AI
assistant adoption (2.53 to 2.76) and 11% after agent adoption (2.76 to 3.08). Jira epics barely move:
the median goes from 0.125 to 0.137 per worker per month for AI assistants, and from 0.137 to 0.157
for AI agents. Finally, the code review distributions point to the mechanism. After agent adoption,
the median firm’s pull requests receive 1.89 comments, compared with 1.21 before, a 57% increase.
There is no such change after AI assistant adoption (1.49 to 1.44).

differ in level from firms that never adopt: for example, firms that adopt AI tools may be more
productive to begin with. Second, outcomes trend over time: for example, firms grow. The difference-
in-differences design below addresses both.

    We are primarily interested in the firm-level effect of access to generative AI tools, rather than an
individual-level effect, for two reasons. First, we are interested in the effects of AI adoption on firm-
level outcomes, including output and employment. In turn, although the effect on coding productivity
can be measured at an individual level, we are primarily interested in the aggregated firm-level
estimate, though we do also report the effects from an instrumented version of our specification in
Section 4.4. Second, the firm-level treatment represents the economically relevant margin for firms
making decisions about whether to adopt generative AI tools.

Identification assumptions. Our identification strategy relies on the assumption that, absent
treatment, earlier- and later-adopting firms would have followed parallel trajectories in outcomes.
The technology adoption process is primarily shaped by two factors: (1) the timing by which firms
learn about AI tools, and (2) the length of firms’ AI procurement processes. We detail this process in
Section 2.4 and Appendix Section C (Rapoport et al., 2025; IBM, 2024).
    We may be concerned that some factors shape both the timing of adoption and firms’ outcome
trajectories. We assess this possibility by first checking for balance on observable characteristics in
Table 4. Here, we observe that firm size — as measured by overall employment in Revelio as well as
engineering employment in Jellyfish — is predictive of take-up timing for both AI coding assistants
and AI coding agents, with larger firms adopting AI earlier. Other observable factors, including
firms’ product type and industry, do not exhibit consistent relationships with take-up timing. This
relationship is consistent with existing empirical evidence on adoption of AI (Yotzov et al., 2026;
Chatterji et al., 2026), as well as with qualitative accounts of the technology adoption process, in
that larger firms are generally more knowledgeable about frontier technologies than smaller firms.
This pattern would pose issues for identification if we also expect that these larger firms would



                                                   29
exhibit different outcome trajectories — for instance, if they are also growing more quickly. The
imbalance in take-up motivates our inclusion of time-varying controls for baseline firm size in our
main specification.
      Beyond firm size, procurement typically involves a number of logistical factors that are plausibly
orthogonal to firms’ growth trajectories, but shape the timing of adoption, as described in Section
2.4 and Appendix Section C. Firms must go through a number of approval layers — business, legal,
security — and often will pilot tools with a small group of workers prior to purchasing a business
license. The lengths of these processes vary significantly across firms, generating quasi-random
variation in timing of adoption, which we leverage for our identification strategy.
      We assess the parallel trends assumption empirically by examining pre-period coefficients,
displayed in the results of the sections below. To the extent that unobserved confounders remain,
we would generally expect them to produce upward bias: factors that accelerate adoption would
also tend to produce higher outcome growth, leading us to overstate treatment effects. However, we
largely find that the effects of AI are quite small, including that they are smaller than those found by
others who study the impacts of AI on software engineering. Furthermore, insofar as any endogeneity
concerns should produce bias in our results, the bias should show up similarly across our event
studies. The focus of the paper is largely on the pass-through of the estimates of effects on coding
output to final software output and employment — and we do not have reason to believe that the
endogeneity concerns should differ across these outcomes.


4.3     Effects on intermediate code output
First, we document the effects of AI adoption on code output. As described before, we examine three
measures of code output: (1) lines of code added, (2) commits, and (3) pull requests opened.
      Figure 3 displays our estimated effects on code output. Panels A and B display event studies for
the estimated effects of AI assistants and AI agents on lines of code added per worker-month. For AI
assistants, twelve months after adoption, the effect is 1,045.9 lines per worker-month, or 30.4% of
the baseline mean of 3,445.3, though this estimate is marginally insignificant. For AI agents, twelve
months after adoption, the effect is 2,113.6 lines per worker-month, or 44.0% of the baseline mean of
4,806.1, and this estimate is also marginally insignificant.
      One concern is that the change in lines of code reflects the verbosity of AI-generated code rather
than a genuine increase in coding output. This would both inflate the measured code output effect
and introduce additional noise into our estimates. To assess this, we also examine the effects on the
number of commits per worker-month in Panels C and D and pull requests per worker-month in
Panels E and F. For commits, the effect for AI assistants is positive and significant: 3.11 commits per
worker-month, or 16.8% of the baseline mean of 18.55. For AI agents, we also find a significant effect

of 9.51 commits per worker-month, or 36.0% of the baseline mean of 26.43.
      Pull requests represent a coarser unit of work than commits, representing larger code changes

requests opened. Put differently, only about 62.7% of the additional pull requests opened following
agent adoption are merged, well below the 86.1% of pull requests merged at baseline.

productivity effects of AI coding assistants against those reported in the broader literature (Cui et
al., 2024; Hoffmann et al., 2024; Song et al., 2024; Sarkar, 2025; Demirer, Musolff, & Yang, 2026). Our
estimates are broadly comparable in magnitude, lending confidence that the results are not driven by
features specific to our sample or design.
      Anecdotal accounts of software engineers’ experiences with AI also support these empirical
findings. For instance, one senior software engineer describes how AI tools have significantly sped
up the process of writing code, particularly on simpler coding tasks:

4.4     Robustness and alternative specifications
We test the robustness of our results to alternative estimators and specifications. Appendix Figure A7
displays the results, focusing on the effects of agent adoption on lines of code added.
      First, a recent literature shows that two-way fixed effects estimates can be biased when adoption is
staggered and treatment effects vary across firms or over time (Borusyak et al., 2024; de Chaisemartin
& D’Haultfœuille, 2024; Callaway & Sant’Anna, 2021; Sun & Abraham, 2021). Panel A compares


                                                         31
our two-way fixed effects estimates with the estimators proposed in these papers. The estimates
are similar across estimators: all show flat pre-periods and an increase in lines of code, with similar
magnitudes of effects and time patterns. This matches a recent survey of published papers, which
finds that heterogeneity-robust estimators rarely overturn the results of standard two-way fixed
effects (Hull, 2026). We therefore use two-way fixed effects for the rest of the paper.
    Second, Panels B–E vary the specification. Twelve months after adoption, the main specification
gives an effect of 44.0% of the baseline mean. Adding the firms that never adopt agents to the
comparison group (Panel B) gives 57.7%. Dropping the controls for baseline firm size and for the
start of each firm’s data coverage (Panel C) gives 38.3%. Panel D restricts the sample of Panel B to
a balanced panel of adopters: firms observed in every month from 8 months before to 12 months
after adoption, so the same firms lie behind every event-time coefficient. This gives 73.8%, with no
evidence of pre-trends (𝑝 = 0.624). We keep the never adopters as controls here because, without
them, the balanced panel has almost no firms left to serve as controls in the last months of the data:
by then, every adopter observed for 12 months after adoption has already adopted. Finally, measuring
lines of code as a firm-level total rather than per worker (Panel E) gives 77.6%. In each case the
pre-period coefficients remain close to zero, and the effect is similar to or larger than in the main
specification, except when we drop the controls, where it is somewhat smaller.

4.6     Effects on employment
Finally, we turn to the effects on employment. If firms do not fully pass through a productivity
gain to increased output, they may instead adjust by cutting employment. We assess the effects on
employment across three margins: (1) on the number of active engineers, as tracked in the JF data,
and on total firm employment, as tracked through a merge with LinkedIn profiles; (2) separately for
junior and senior workers; and (3) on the margins of hires versus separations.
      We begin with the number of engineers active in the JF data in each firm-month, displayed in
Figure 5. Neither AI assistants nor AI agents have a significant effect on engineering employment.
However, these estimates are somewhat noisy — we can only rule out effects outside of −4.7% to
10.7% for assistants, and −15.6% to 10.5% for agents.
      Next, we assess effects on the firm’s total employment, as measured by the total number of
LinkedIn profiles attached to the firm, in Appendix Figure A16. This measure allows us to assess
effects on both engineering and non-engineering teams. It also allows us to assess heterogeneity
by seniority, without issues of differential coverage by seniority. Both panels display zero effects.
These estimates are more precise — we can rule out effects outside of −4.5% to 5.1% for assistants,
and −2.5% to 10.3% for agents.


                                                    34
    It is possible that the aggregate effect masks heterogeneity by worker seniority. Some papers have
found employment declines among junior workers and gains among senior workers (Brynjolfsson,
Chandar, & Chen, 2025; Lichtinger & Hosseini Maasoum, 2025). We test for this using the LinkedIn
data on junior and senior workers in the firm. The results are displayed in Appendix Figure A17. We
do not find significant effects of either AI assistants or AI agents on junior or senior workers.
    Finally, it is also possible that these aggregate effects mask churn in employment. In particular,
the papers that have found employment declines have generally found them on the margin of reduced
hiring. We assess this possibility using the LinkedIn data, measuring hires as the number of workers
starting their first position at the firm each month, and separations as the number of workers ending
their last position at the firm each month. Appendix Figure A16 displays the results for overall hires
and separations, and Appendix Figures A18 and A19 display them separately for junior and senior
workers. We find no evidence of a decline in hiring after either tool’s adoption. AI assistants show a
modest increase in hiring, driven by senior workers, and an insignificant change in separations. AI
agents show null effects on both hires and separations. In particular, we do not find the decline in
junior hiring documented in prior work. The one exception to the null effects on separations is a
marginally significant increase in senior separations after AI agent adoption.
    These null effects on employment are consistent with some empirical work documenting small
employment effects of generative AI (Humlum & Vestergaard, 2025), though some papers find evidence
of much larger effects (Brynjolfsson, Chandar, & Chen, 2025; Lichtinger & Hosseini Maasoum, 2025).
We interpret the results of our staggered AI adoption design to rule out an employment decline that
would result from a direct automation effect. In other words, we rule out the possibility that firms
have integrated AI tools sufficiently into their workflows that they no longer need workers.
    However, we also emphasize several caveats when interpreting our results. First, we present
a reasonably short-run estimate of the effect of generative AI on employment; it is plausible that
employment responds slowly to technological changes. Section 6 assesses potential future trajectories
in employment outcomes. Second, our event study design identifies the partial equilibrium effect of
generative AI adoption. It is plausible that this null partial equilibrium effect masks a negative general
equilibrium effect. To see this possibility, note that giving a single firm access to a labor-augmenting
technology will have two effects in partial equilibrium: first, there will be a displacement effect, since
the number of workers per unit of output will fall; and, second, there will be a scale effect, as the
output of the firm may increase (Acemoglu & Restrepo, 2018). This scale effect may be primarily
business-stealing from other firms. If so, we would expect reductions in those firms’ employment.

5     Production bottlenecks
We observe significant effects of AI coding tools on intermediate measures of code output, but we do
not observe significant effects on downstream outcomes, including software output or employment.
How can we reconcile such findings? In this section, we provide evidence on production bottlenecks
as a mechanism: if other steps in the software production process are slow to adjust, or negatively
affected, productivity gains in code writing may not pass through to firm-level output. Our data
provides us with particularly granular insights into the review process, which we focus on as a
bottleneck.


5.1     Descriptives about the code review process
Software production requires a series of sequential complementary tasks, as described in Section
2. While AI raises productivity in code writing, other tasks may serve as limiting factors. In this
subsection, we test whether code review serves as such a bottleneck.
      Once a block of code is ready for review, the engineer will submit a GitHub pull request. One
or several other team members will read through and perhaps write tests on the code, to assess
whether the code meets the necessary usage requirements and integrates correctly into the rest of
the codebase. If necessary, they will leave comments and submit a request for changes. The code
writer and code reviewers will iterate through these changes until the code is ready to be combined
into the codebase, at which point they will “merge” the pull request.

Workers’ time allocation. To empirically test the relevance of production bottlenecks, we begin
by documenting the relative breakdown of an engineer’s time allocation into these distinct tasks in
Figure 6 Panel A. Column 1 shows the results, imputing time allocation from the Jellyfish data. We
convert the panel of work signals (GitHub activity, Jira activity, and Google Calendar activity) into
relative time allocation on coding, code review, and management. With this approach, we observe
that engineers spend only 40.8% of their time writing code. Column 2 shows the results from a survey
of 100 Prolific engineers on their average weekly time breakdown. On average, engineers spend only
30.8% of their time writing code.

Substitutability of workers. Next, we provide evidence regarding the substitutability of workers
between coding and review, in support of our modeling assumption of a review ceiling. Although firms
do not hire completely separate code writers and reviewers, workers are not perfectly substitutable
across these tasks. First, Figure 6 Panel B provides evidence on reviewers’ familiarity with code.
Reviewers mostly review code they already know: 82.8% of reviews are in a code base where the



                                                 36
reviewer had previously committed code or opened a pull request. If each review had instead gone
to a random pull request opened at the same firm in the same month, only 17.2% would have been.
Reviewing draws on knowledge of a specific code base, so a worker cannot be moved onto reviewing
any pull request at no cost. Second, Panel C provides evidence on the seniority of reviewers, separately
by seniority of the code author. Senior engineers do 75.7% of the reviewing on pull requests written
by senior engineers, but only 32.8% on pull requests written by junior engineers. Put the other way,
junior engineers do only 24.3% of the reviewing of senior engineers’ code.


5.2       Effects on review

the number of days between when a pull request is submitted and when it is merged. AI assistants

of effect to the fact that the impacts on coding activity, from the previous section, are only moderately
large. By contrast, AI agents significantly lengthen the review process, by 1.99 days, or 56.1% of the
baseline mean of 3.56 days.

top tercile of repositories, time to merge rises by 69.7% — against 25.4% in the bottom tercile — while
pull requests per worker rise by 30.0%, against 64.3%. Branches give a similar gradient (Appendix
Figure A28, Panel B). The effects on Jira issues resolved are similar across terciles.

6.2       Results and test of model fit
The results of our estimation are reported in Table 5. First, our estimation allows us to assess the
reduced form estimates’ implied changes in coding and review productivity. Coding productivity
increases significantly, at 73.7%. This is larger than what is implied by the pull requests created,
because labor is being reallocated away from coding at the same time: the same flow of created
requests is produced by fewer coder-hours. Additionally, review productivity decreases slightly, at
−7.0%, which comes from the increase in the share of requests that draw a comment, though the
effect is marginally insignificant.
      The estimates also provide implied values of the intensive margin of labor reallocation. They
indicate a reallocation away from coding labor of −22.7% (−14.9 percentage points from a baseline

6.3       Future impacts
Our empirical estimates provide us with evidence on the short-run impacts of AI on productivity,
output, and employment, with current versions of AI tools and short-run firm adjustment. However,
we are also interested in how AI will impact these outcomes in the future. With the estimated model,
we explore counterfactual changes in outcomes: first, changes from additional firm adjustment;
second, changes from advancements in AI’s technical capabilities. In doing so, we follow recent work
that uses models of production to assess the economic implications of advances in AI (e.g., C. Jones,
2026; C. Jones & Tonetti, 2026; B. Jones, 2025; Korinek et al., 2026).

6.3.1     Firm adjustment

We explore two ways in which the firm may continue to adjust, even without additional advancements
in AI capabilities. First, we explore what additional adjustment is possible with only internal labor
reallocation, and without any additional hiring or labor displacement. Second, we explore how the
firm may choose to respond in the longer run with more flexible labor adjustment and output choice
— i.e., if we relax the assumption that overall labor is held fixed.

Medium-run adjustment, with fixed overall labor. To assess how much the firm can adjust

employment of 33.2%, while review labor still increases by 24.9% — demonstrating that the labor

with fewer engineers, and that engineering work shifts towards review rather than code writing.

6.3.2   Advances in AI

The previous section explored how the firm may continue to adjust, holding the technology fixed. In
this section, we explore how advances in AI’s technical capabilities may shape the trajectories of

coding productivity, output, and employment.

Trajectories of technical capabilities and coding productivity. We begin by projecting a trajec-
tory for coding productivity 𝐴𝐶 . Our estimates give us two points on this trajectory. AI assistants,
adopted by the median firm in April 2024, raise 𝐴𝐶 by a factor of 1.35 relative to the pre-AI period; AI
agents, adopted by the median firm in May 2025, raise it by a further factor of 1.74, for a cumulative
factor of 2.34.8 To extend this trajectory beyond our two estimates, we need to take a stance on its
shape — e.g., linear versus exponential. We draw on recent work in computer science that tracks the
technical capabilities of frontier AI models over time. In particular, Kwa et al. (2025) measure the
length of software tasks — in terms of the time a human expert takes to complete them — that frontier
models can complete with 50% reliability. They find that this time horizon has grown exponentially
(i.e., linear in logs), doubling roughly every 129 days for models released since 2023, and use this
trend to forecast future capabilities. We therefore fit the same type of curve to 𝐴𝐶 : we assume that
𝐴𝐶 similarly grows exponentially — i.e., that ln 𝐴𝐶 rises linearly over time, at a different rate — and
fit this curve through our two estimates of the productivity gains from adoption of AI assistants and
AI agents. Appendix Section G.3.2 describes this construction in detail.
        Figure 10 Panel A plots the results of this exercise. The grey line shows the time horizon of
frontier models relative to its level in February 2023, with METR’s measurements marked; the red
line shows the fitted path for 𝐴𝐶 , with both extrapolated through 2031. Two things stand out. First,
the slope of technical capabilities on lab benchmarks is far steeper than the slope of productivity
gains in practice. Between the median adoption dates of AI assistants and AI agents, the time horizon
rises 8.43-fold, while coding productivity rises 1.74-fold. Second, if technical capabilities continue to
improve along this trajectory, 𝐴𝐶 would rise to 8.93 times its pre-AI level by 2028 and 41 times by
2031.
        These are very large numbers. However, they are not unprecedented relative to earlier techno-
logical changes. For example, computing performance improved at an average rate of roughly 45%
per year in the decades after 1945 (Nordhaus, 2007). In cotton spinning, the labor needed to spin 100
lb of fine yarn fell from over 50,000 hours by hand to about 2,000 hours on Crompton’s mule in 1780,
and to about 300 hours by 1795 (Catling, 1970; Chapman, 1972).

to an employment cut instead. Second, consistent with review acting as a bottleneck, the trajectories
depend heavily on whether review productivity improves. With 𝐴𝑅 fixed, output levels off at about
57.8% above its pre-AI level by late 2027. By late 2028, the share of labor devoted to review reaches its
ceiling, and further gains in coding productivity add only to the backlog of unreviewed code. With
𝐴𝑅 growing as fast as 𝐴𝐶 , by contrast, output keeps rising — by 140.9% by the end of 2026 alone.

by 10.3% by 2031, and stops falling once the review ceiling binds: beyond that point, further gains in
coding productivity no longer reduce the labor needed per unit of output. With 𝐴𝑅 growing as fast
as 𝐴𝐶 , each unit of output requires less labor in both coding and review, and employment falls by
76.2% by 2031.
    We view these scenarios as bounds on the long-run impacts of AI. The scenario with 𝐴𝑅 fixed is
one in which AI can do only approximately half of software work — writing code — while the rest of
the work still requires human labor. The scenario with 𝐴𝑅 growing as fast as 𝐴𝐶 is one in which AI
can do essentially all of software engineering work, since in our model, software production involves
only these two inputs. The magnitudes in this second scenario are therefore very large, and should be
read as an upper bound. In practice, software production also involves tasks that our model abstracts
from — e.g., deciding what to build, testing, and coordinating with customers — which would act as
further bottlenecks.

7    Conclusion
Generative AI has garnered significant attention, yet its impacts on real-world production settings
are still poorly understood. This paper studies firm-level adoption of two generations of AI coding
tools — AI assistants and AI agents — using 445 million work events from an engineering analytics
platform, which allow us to observe the full software production process, from code writing to code
review to final software output.
    We find that AI tools substantially increase intermediate code output, but these gains do not
translate into proportional changes in final software output or employment. Following agent adoption,
pull requests opened increase by 35.7%, while Jira issues resolved increase by only 13.7%, and we
can rule out increases larger than 30.7%. We cannot attribute significant changes in engineering
employment to either generation of tools.
    Code review explains much of this gap. After agent adoption, the time from a pull request’s
submission to its merge increases by 56.1%. Code waits longer for review — consistent with a capacity
constraint — and spends longer in active review and draws more comments — consistent with a
decline in code quality. Estimating our model with these estimates as moments, we find that agents
raise coding productivity by 73.7%, and that firms shift labor from coding towards review.
    The estimated model also suggests that these short-run effects are not the end of the story. Once
firms adjust employment, the model predicts that output rises by 16.1% and employment falls by
13.2%. With continued technological advancement, the long-run effects depend crucially on whether
AI raises review productivity as quickly as coding productivity: if it does not, output rises by 57.8%
by 2031; if it does, output rises by up to 341.4% and employment falls by up to 76.2%.
    These findings have several implications for firms. First, AI’s productivity impacts are uneven
across tasks, even within an occupation like software engineering that is considered highly au-
tomatable. Thus, measuring the returns to AI requires evaluating the entire production process:
intermediate metrics, such as lines of code or commits, overstate the gains from AI adoption. Second,
realizing these gains requires complementary organizational investments. As one stage of production
speeds up, the bottleneck shifts to another, and labor must be reallocated towards it.
    Our results are subject to several qualifications. First, we estimate effects over the first 12 to
16 months after adoption, and employment may respond slowly to technological change. Second,
our design identifies partial equilibrium effects; if output gains come partly from business-stealing,
employment at other firms may fall. Third, our sample consists largely of medium-to-large, well-
established firms, and effects may differ for start-ups or in other knowledge work settings.
    More broadly, our findings suggest that the economics of AI adoption cannot be understood by
studying automated tasks in isolation. As with previous waves of technological change, the impacts


                                                 47
of AI depend not only on the technology but also on how quickly firms reorganize production around
it. Understanding where bottlenecks arise — and how firms should reorganize to address them — will
be central to understanding the long-run effects of AI on productivity, organizational design, and
labor demand.

C      Qualitative interviews
To assess the validity of our identification strategy and empirical interpretation, we conduct a series
of qualitative interviews with software engineers, engineering managers, product managers, and
related stakeholders across a range of firms. In total, we interview 21 individuals spanning different
roles, seniority levels, and company types.
      The interviews provide context on firms’ AI adoption processes, the organization of software
production, and the mechanisms through which AI coding tools affect productivity, bottlenecks, and
product development. Quotes have been lightly edited for clarity.


C.1     AI adoption
Procurement Process

“We’ve got four teams involved in purchasing any tech. One is on the sourcing side, and handles large
contracts; they understand whether the contracts are financially favorable towards [the company]. You
also have legal, who will have an opinion on whether [the company’s] data and IP are protected. Security
gets involved as well. Finally, we expect IT to own and operate the tech centrally. The process typically
takes a few weeks minimum. That can also mean months. ChatGPT came out in November 2022. Fall of
2023 is when we initially acquired a few test licenses before we rolled it out.”
                                                                    — Head of AI Analytics and Insights


“Our EA just asked IT and legal if it was ok to get it and got approval fast. Usually, this happens within a
day for us.”                                                                       — Engineering Manager


“There is an intake procurement form, and the procurement manager will go through various assessments.
We go through quality assessments, cybersecurity assessments, and assessments about compliance. So on
average, the approval will take anywhere between six and twelve months.”
                                                             — Associate Director of Strategic Planning



Are workers allowed to use tools through individual licenses?
“Our code is our intellectual property. What happens to the code that’s exposed to GitHub Copilot? ... We
said, ‘do not use anything that is not approved by [the company].’ ”
                                                                    — Head of AI Analytics and Insights



                                                    65
“No, that is not allowed.”                                                       — Engineering Manager



How was the adoption decision related to the firm’s expansion plans?

“[Our company] is constantly in a growth stage, because unfortunately our shareholders care about it on
a quarterly basis.”                                          — Associate Director of Strategic Planning


C.2     Effects of AI
Software production process

“We have our own product insights from seeing what customers are lacking, and then we also have direct
customer requests where they say, ‘hey, I need a button here that does this.’ We bring all of those insights
together and prioritize them using what is called a RICE score, looking at the level of effort, the impact
we think it will have, and the reach — how many customers it would affect.”
                                                      — Startup Founder and Former Product Manager


“Every quarter, your team does quarterly planning and decides on a bucket of projects for the quarter.
You go through and assign time estimates to each task and project. Then within the quarter, every two
weeks, we do sprint planning.”
                                                                             — Senior Software Engineer


“In an ideal team situation, around 80% of the team’s effort is planned work, while 20% is focused on
fixing issues, bugs, or other things that come up unexpectedly.” — Startup Founder and Former Product
Manager


“Work typically moves through the Jira statuses of ‘Implementation’, ‘Review’, ‘QA Testing’, and then
‘Release’. For a task to get marked as completed, it not only has to be merged, but also deployed to
production.”
                                                                             — Junior Software Engineer


“After the engineer finishes building the solution, it goes into the QA process and user acceptance testing.
QA ensures that the new code has not broken the codebase or introduced inconsistencies, while user
acceptance testing ensures that the ticket actually satisfies the intended business requirements. Once both
of those are complete, the changes move into the release candidate.”
                                                      — Startup Founder and Former Product Manager


                                                    66
“We only resolve a Jira issue once we’ve deployed the code associated with it to production.”
                                                                              — Senior Software Engineer


Coding productivity

“For simple and routine tasks, there’s so much documentation, so the models are very well-trained. With
Claude Code, I can write a draft in two seconds and it’s going to be right 90% of the time; I just have to
do a little bit of testing myself. There is a huge speed-up, on the order of 100x. But for deeper engineering
tasks, it doesn’t really save as much time — it gets stuff wrong a lot, and you end up spending more time
in the review process.”                                                       — Senior Software Engineer


“I use LLMs for nearly every task now. I do not actually type much code anymore. But I also have over a
decade of software design principles, systems knowledge, and intuition.” — Senior Software Engineer


“The model gets you maybe 95% of the way there, and then the remaining 5% is figuring out what it got
wrong and fixing it. My work became less about writing code directly and more about reviewing and
correcting what Claude produced. But it still sped up my coding work substantially.”
                                                                              — Junior Software Engineer




Production bottlenecks

“We were able to write code faster in some instances, but there is only one bottleneck at a time. If you
speed up coding, the bottleneck shifts somewhere else. In most organizations I have worked in, the real
bottleneck has been communication between business and product teams, as well as review and QA. You
can generate features much faster now, but they still need to be validated and approved by humans.”
                                                                              — Senior Software Engineer


“People could make a huge number of commits quickly, but code review was still the bottleneck. On
one project, only one senior engineer was allowed to approve changes because he was the expert on the
codebase. He was so bandwidth constrained that he could only review my code once a week. After some
outages that were caused by AI-generated commits, there were also new rules requiring senior engineer
approval for AI-generated code, which created even more review bottlenecks.”
                                                                              — Junior Software Engineer


“A lot of business-value tickets are constrained by things other than coding itself. On one project, I spent

six months waiting for legal approval for something that probably should have taken a month. Coding
agents can dramatically increase coding productivity, but Jira tickets often capture bottlenecks that AI
cannot solve.”
                                                                            — Senior Software Engineer


Review process

“Now, pull requests are getting bigger and harder to review. It is honestly becoming more anxiety inducing
to even open the pull request and begin the review.”
                                                                            — Senior Software Engineer


“Even with AI tools, you absolutely still need humans involved in the review process. I don’t see a time
where you will ever not need human oversight, even as tools get better. The AI tools lack the comprehension
of the overview of the entire feature, and what it is supposed to do.”
                                                                            — Senior Software Engineer


“It’s typically our senior engineers who do reviews. It’s about having ownership over the code and the
impacts of shipping buggy code.”
                                                                            — Senior Software Engineer


“When teams assign code review, assignment usually boils down to availability. But, if a PR or area of
code is complex, I expect at least one reviewer to be senior.”
                                                                            — Senior Software Engineer

F.1     Measuring AI take-up
We construct firm- and individual-level AI tool adoption dates by combining two sources: (1) API
data on business license seat activations collected by JF, and (2) signals derived from GitHub commit
and pull request data.

Source 1: API data. JF’s platform integrates directly with the APIs of three AI coding tools —
GitHub Copilot, Cursor, and Claude Code. For each integration, the API provides the date at which
the worker first uses the AI tool. We take the minimum of these dates as the first firm-level usage.
      Tool codes in the raw data are mapped to two treatment groups as follows: AI coding assistants
include GitHub Copilot (GC) and Cursor (CURS) (truncated to take-up measured pre-July 2024, to
avoid conflation with take-up of AI coding agents); AI coding agents include Claude Code.

Source 2: GitHub signals. We extract three types of signals from the GitHub data.
      Signal A — Bot account detection. We match GitHub user login and name fields against patterns
for over 70 known AI tools using regular expressions. Each matched account is assigned to a tool
name and category. The date of first appearance of a bot account of a given category within a firm
provides the earliest signal of that tool type’s use. The table below lists representative tools and the
account identifiers used to match them.

F.2    Measuring Jira issue content
In this section, we describe our method for classifying Jira tasks based on their text descriptions.
Each Jira task contains a “summary”, a “description”, a user-inputted “issue type”, linked “project
name”, and linked “parent task”. This task information can be manually entered by a worker (the “task
creator”) or automatically generated (e.g., an automatic bug report). On average, the text descriptions
are 580 characters long, amounting to roughly one paragraph.

LLM labels. We begin by using the OpenAI API to classify a 0.5% random sample of tasks, stratified
by company, on the estimated time an experienced software engineer would require to complete the
task (length). We use the GPT-4o model with the following prompt:
You are a Jira task classification AI.
For each provided software engineering task, classify it according to the "length"
label defined below. Assume the perspective of a software engineer completing the
task, inferring implicit steps and context.


Length: The number of hours that an experienced software engineer
(i.e., 5+ years of work experience) would require to complete the task,
without the assistance of generative AI tools.
- 0 = Short: < 4 hours. Low complexity, routine, single-component change.
- 1 = Medium: >= 4 and < 8 hours. Medium complexity, multi-component change.
- 2 = Long: >= 8 hours and < 12 hours. High complexity, cross-team change.
- 3 = Very Long: >= 12 hours. Very high complexity, large scope change.


As output, provide the label in the following JSON format:
{"length": <int>}


Below is information about the task, including context about its parent task and project.
Classify the task itself, but use the context to inform your classification.
Project name: {project_name}
Parent task summary: {parent_task}
Task type: {issue_type}
Task summary: {summary}
Task description: {description}



Supervised extrapolation. We use a supervised learning approach to scale the GPT-assigned labels
to the full dataset. First, we convert the Jira issue text descriptions into numerical embeddings
using the Sentence-BERT (SBERT) model all-MiniLM-L6-v2, a transformer-based architecture
designed for sentence-level encoding. Specifically, the model maps each issue’s text description
(summary and description) into a 384-dimensional numerical vector. Second, we train a neural
network to predict the GPT-assigned length label from the embeddings, using PyTorch. We perform
this training on the pre-AI adoption set of Jira issues, and then run the trained model on the entire
set of Jira issues.
